Toes to scalp: Skin cancer can show up in unexpected spots

Knowing what and where to look for signs of skin cancer

Beth N. McLellan, MD, Chief of the Division of Dermatology, Montefiore Einstein
June 7, 2022, 11:56 a.m. ET
EN ESPAÑOL: De los pies al cuero cabelludo: el cáncer de piel puede aparecer en lugares inesperados

It’s easy to assume that skin cancer happens only on parts of the body that are regularly in the sun, like your face, arms and neck. But it’s important to know that skin cancer may appear anywhere on the body, including in surprising spots that don’t get much sun, like the soles of your feet, the scalp, deep in your armpits and under your finger and toe nails.

Skin cancer is the most diagnosed cancer. Too much sun with not enough sunscreen puts everyone at a higher risk, regardless of age, gender or ethnicity. A history of childhood sunburns is another risk factor, no matter how careful you are as an adult. Two types of skin cancer — basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma — are very common. They may initially look like a non-healing pimple or a dry, rough patch, but if those types of lesions don’t return to normal within three to four weeks, have a dermatologist check it out.

Melanoma is a more serious, yet less common, form of skin cancer that often appears as new or changing brown spots. Those spots can even show up under the finger and toe nails. And although people who have fair skin and are more susceptible to sun burn can be at higher risk, individuals with darker skin are still at risk. 

Adding self-checks to annual dermatologist skin checks

While it is recommended to see a dermatologist for a full-body skin check once a year, because dermatologists have a well-trained eye and can check hard-to-view places like the back of your neck and seemingly hidden patches, it is also recommended to perform monthly self-checks in between visits. 

Perform these monthly at-home skin checks in front of a full-length mirror. Look at your front and back with your arms raised so you won’t miss anything. Check under your arms, the soles of your feet, the palms of your hands and between your toes. Use a hand mirror to examine the back of your neck and as much of your scalp as possible. You might want to enlist a partner or friend to take a look at your back and scalp. For those who wear nail polish, make sure to take a quick look for any new marks under your finger and toe nails before you apply a new coat of polish.

Dermatologists suggest looking for the ABCDEs of skin cancer when evaluating a suspicious lesion: Asymmetrical or abnormally shaped moles, irregular Borders, differences in Color, a Diameter larger than a pencil eraser, and Evolution, or changes in size, shape or color. Evolution may be the most important warning sign.

The more often you examine your skin, the more familiar you’ll become with what’s normal on your body. Have your dermatologist check out anything that looks bigger, darker or different than your other spots — or if it’s evolved in the past month.

Self-checks are especially important for those at higher risk of skin cancer such as people who are immune compromised, people who have had skin cancer before and people with a strong family history of skin cancer.

If you don’t have a dermatologist, please talk to your primary care physician about annual skin checks and a referral to a dermatologist. 

Five things you can do to lower your risk of skin cancer

The steps you can take to lower your risk of skin cancer may not be new to you, but they have proven effective at reducing risk.

Up to 90% of skin cancers are directly attributable to exposure to sunlight or artificial tanning lights. The ultraviolet A (UVA) and ultraviolet B (UVB) components of the sun’s radiation are the culprits. UVA causes wrinkles and premature aging of the skin while UVB causes burns, and both of them can lead to cancer. A combination of strategies to avoid exposure to UVA and UVB is outlined in the 5 S’s. 

Slip on a t-shirt

Slop on a broad-spectrum sunscreen

Slap on a broad-brimmed hat

Seek shade whenever possible

Slide on quality sunglasses

The 5 S’s were originally created in Australia in response to a dramatic rise in cases of melanoma. After almost 30 years, declining skin cancer rates have proven the effectiveness of this combination of measures, all of which come down to blocking harmful UVA and UVB rays.

Use sunscreen with “broad spectrum” on the label, for protection against UVA and UVB radiation. Seek products with at least SPF 30. Reapply every two hours. For people with more sensitive skin, consider using physical or mineral blocker SPF ingredients, such as zinc oxide and/or titanium oxide.

Physically blocking the sun with clothing, hats and sunglasses is an easy way to protect yourself. Specially designed clothing is increasingly available with a much higher ultraviolet protection factor (UPF) than regular street clothes, and this can be especially helpful for days spent outdoors at the beach, pool or on the water. There is even a laundry additive that you can add that will increase the UPF of your regular clothes.

Sun protection is important year-round, even during winter. Make it easy by choosing a moisturizer with sunscreen, so you do everything in one step.

Remember, sunscreen is for everyone, not just for people with fair skin. Not only does it decrease your risk of skin cancer, it also protects against dark spots, wrinkles and other signs of aging.
