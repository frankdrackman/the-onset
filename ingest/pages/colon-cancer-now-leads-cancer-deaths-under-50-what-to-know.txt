Colon cancer now leads cancer deaths under 50: What to know

By Chaoyuan Kuang, MD, PhD, Co-Leader, GI Clinical and Translational Research, Montefiore Einstein Comprehensive Cancer Center, for Montefiore Einstein

EN ESPAÑOL: Cáncer de colon, 1ª causa de muerte en adultos menores de 50: Qué saber

Colorectal cancer is now the leading cause of cancer-related death among adults under 50 in the United States, and diagnoses in younger adults continue to increase at an alarming rate. In fact, it has been rising for more than a decade — a shift that has caught many experts by surprise. Historically considered a disease that primarily affected older adults, colon cancer is now being diagnosed more frequently in younger people, often without a known family history or genetic risk.

This shift is raising new concerns about how lifestyle and diet may be affecting cancer risk. While no single cause explains the rise in colorectal cancer among people in their 20s, 30s, and 40s, a growing body of research has linked diets high in ultra-processed foods, added sugars, and unhealthy fats to increased inflammation in the body — an environment that may promote cancer development. Researchers also continue to take a closer look at factors like sedentary habits, changes in the gut microbiome, and other environmental exposures as possible contributors.

This trend is especially concerning because many cases in younger adults are diagnosed at later stages, when treatment is more complex and outcomes may be poorer. Symptoms often don’t appear, or aren’t recognized, until the disease is beyond its earliest stage. Because colon cancer can grow quietly for years, timely screening and lifestyle changes to reduce risk are critical, even for those not seeing themselves at risk. Here’s what to know about your risk — and the steps you can take to help prevent colon cancer or catch it early.

STOP COLON CANCER BEFORE IT STARTS

Colorectal cancer is often preventable, and there are proven steps you can take to reduce your risk. Screenings allow doctors to find colorectal cancer early, when it’s easiest to treat. When caught early, colon cancer has a survival rate higher than 90% — and it may even be preventable. Some screening methods, such as colonoscopy, can also prevent cancer by identifying and removing polyps and other precancerous growths. Beyond screening, there are several other important steps that can help lower your risk — here are six that matter most:

Maintain a balanced diet.

The so-called “Western diet” tends to be low in fiber and high in red and processed meats and unhealthy fats, has been associated with a higher risk of colorectal cancer. By contrast, choosing foods that support your gut instead of stressing it can make a real difference.  Focusing on a balanced, colorful diet rich in fiber from fruits, vegetables, and whole grains can help support your digestive health.

Limit ultra-processed foods.

Foods such as candy, soda, and white bread, along with items high in added sugars and artificial ingredients — such as sweetened breakfast cereals and packaged snacks — have been linked to an increased risk of colorectal cancer.

Stay physically active.

Regular activity — even daily walking — supports your overall health and helps keep your gut working well. In one recent study, regular exercise was shown to reduce the risk of colorectal cancer by approximately 20%.

Maintain a healthy weight.

Excess body fat, especially around the abdomen, is associated with a higher risk of colorectal cancer.

Avoid smoking and vaping.

Tobacco in any form is hard on your body. Smoking damages cells and is linked to many cancers, including colorectal cancer. And vaping isn’t a “safer” workaround — most vape products still deliver nicotine and other chemicals that inflame and injure your lungs and blood vessels, adding health risks that researchers are still uncovering

Limit alcohol use.

Moderate to heavy drinking increases risk; if you drink, limit intake to one drink per day for women and two for men.

Family history & health factors that influence your risk

In addition to lifestyle factors, certain personal and genetic factors can also affect your risk of colorectal cancer — and help determine when you should begin screening.

A personal or family history of colorectal cancer or colon polyps: An individual’s risk for developing colorectal cancer is two to three times greater when a parent, sibling, or child has the disease, and the risk goes up to six times greater when two such close relatives have colorectal cancer. Lynch syndrome is a hereditary gene disorder that is associated with a significant increased risk for different types of cancer, including colorectal cancer. If an immediate family member has colorectal cancer, your doctor may recommend that you start screening at age 40 or 10 years earlier than the age at which the family member was diagnosed.

Racial and ethnic background: People who are of African, Hispanic, or Ashkenazi Jewish descent are at increased risk for colorectal cancer. In fact, Black Americans have the highest colorectal cancer incidence and mortality rates of all racial groups in the country. Colorectal cancer diagnosed before age 50 occurs up to two times more frequently in Black and Hispanic people than in people of European descent.

Metabolic and inflammatory conditions: Conditions that affect metabolism and gut health may also play a role in colorectal cancer risk. Inflammatory bowel diseases such as Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis cause ongoing inflammation in the digestive tract, while conditions like type 2 diabetes are linked to chronic, low-grade inflammation. Over time, this persistent inflammation may contribute to changes in the colon that increase cancer risk.

When to get screened — and why it matters

Screening remains one of the most effective tools for preventing colorectal cancer or catching it early, when it’s most treatable. Current guidelines for people at average risk (no personal or strong family history of colorectal cancer or certain polyps, and no genetic or inflammatory bowel diseases) should begin screening at age 45 — though some experts are now evaluating whether that age should be lowered further in response to rising rates among younger people.

For those with a family history of colorectal cancer or other risk factors, screening may need to begin earlier. Options such as colonoscopy and stool-based tests can detect early signs of cancer — often before symptoms appear — making timely screening a critical part of early detection.

Screening options for detecting — and in some cases preventing — colorectal cancer

Colonoscopy: Colonoscopy is recommended for those at high risk and for those who want to prevent colon cancer, as it allows doctors to find and remove precancerous polyps to stop colon cancer before it starts. It can also identify colorectal cancer at its earliest stages, when it’s most treatable.

Virtual colonoscopy (also known as CT colonography):  Virtual colonoscopy is a noninvasive test that does not require sedation, allowing people to drive themselves home afterwards and even go back to work. It is an effective imaging tool that uses an advanced CT (computerized tomography) scan to generate a 3D image of the colon and surrounding organs. Radiologists look for any potential abnormalities, and if polyps are found, they can be removed during a traditional colonoscopy before they become cancer. Like the traditional colonoscopy, virtual colonoscopy can detect colon cancer at its earliest stages, when it is most treatable.

Testing options for those at low or average risk and with no symptoms

Stool tests and the new blood test are colon cancer screening options for those at low or average risk, offering more convenient and accessible alternatives. These tests help reach people that might otherwise skip screening, improving early detection and saving lives.

Blood testing: The new blood test is the latest advancement in colon cancer testing. It screens for colon cancer by detecting changes in DNA. The first colon cancer blood test was approved in July 2024, and others are in development. This test is appropriate for people at low or average risk of colorectal cancer who do not have any symptoms. It requires only a blood draw, which can be taken during routine blood panels ordered by healthcare providers. If test results are positive, a colonoscopy will be needed for definitive diagnosis.

Stool-based tests: Stool-based tests, available from your doctor,can be done in the comfort of one’s home. There are three types of stool-based tests, and like the blood test, each one can be a first option for individuals who are at low or average risk for colorectal cancer and have no symptoms.If a stool-based test is positive, a colonoscopy must then be performed.

FOBT (fecal occult blood test): There are two types of FOBT that screen for colon cancer through the detection of blood in the stool. The gFOBT (guaiac-based fecal occult blood test) detects blood in the stool using a chemical called guaiac. The iFOBT, also known as the FIT (fecal immunochemical test), uses special antibodies to test the stool for blood. One advantage of the iFOBT/FIT is that it does not require a restriction in diet or medicines. Blood in the stool is a possible early sign of cancer.
Multi-targeted stool test: This test identifies the presence of DNA mutations in the cells of a stool sample. If DNA mutations are detected, this could be an early sign of colorectal cancer.

Don’t ignore these symptoms — call your doctor

Knowing the symptoms of colorectal cancer can help with earlier detection. In addition to staying on top of screenings, people of all ages should pay attention to changes in their bodies. Any change in the size, shape, color, consistency, or frequency of bowel movements — or the presence of bright red or black blood in the stool — should be discussed with your doctor as soon as possible. Other signs, such as persistent abdominal pain, unexplained weight loss, or shifts in bowel habits, should also prompt a conversation with your doctor.

New treatment options are improving life after diagnosis

If colon cancer is found, laparoscopic and robotic tools now support less invasive surgery for tumors at all stages, requiring smaller incisions and allowing successful cancer removal while preserving more of the colon. This type of surgery also leads to faster recovery time, allowing people to more quickly return to work or their daily routines. Some people can even go home the day of their colon cancer surgery.

Precision oncology has also evolved dramatically, allowing doctors to analyze the DNA in a person’s tumor and design a personalized treatment plan that can shrink or stop the cancer. With newer chemotherapy drugs and by using older drugs in new ways, doctors can provide more effective results with fewer side effects, so people can maintain more of their normal activities during treatment. For some people with rectal cancer, a combination of radiation and chemotherapy can lead to complete clinical response, where the tumor is no longer detectable. At that point, the site can be monitored, with no immediate surgery required.

Why awareness matters now

As colorectal cancer continues to rise among younger adults, awareness, prevention, and early detection are more important than ever. This increase is already being seen locally. In the Hudson Valley, Westchester and across New York City, doctors are seeing more younger patients being diagnosed with colorectal cancer — often at later stages.

While colorectal cancer risk is influenced by a range of factors — from lifestyle to family history and overall health — many are within a person’s control. Screening offers something different: the ability to detect cancer early or prevent it. Staying up to date on screenings, recognizing warning signs, and making small, sustainable changes can help protect long-term health.
