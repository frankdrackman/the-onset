How more people are beating lung cancer today

Tamar Nobel, MD, Thoracic Surgeon, Montefiore Einstein Comprehensive Cancer Center, for Montefiore Einstein

EN ESPAÑOL: Por qué cada vez más personas superan el cáncer de pulmón

Lung cancer care today looks very different from how it did just a few years ago. Those who once had few options now have much greater hope thanks to earlier detection and more precise, less invasive procedures. Along with these advancements, more and more people are benefiting from breakthrough therapies tailored to their unique tumor features. This shift is making it possible to not just survive lung cancer but also recover faster and return to their daily lives.

The wave of innovations helping people beat lung cancer — including AI-assisted CT screening, minimally invasive robotic surgeries, and precision therapies — is fundamentally reshaping care. Immune-based treatments, like checkpoint inhibitors, are supercharging the immune system’s ability to directly combat cancer, paving the way for quicker recoveries and giving new hope to patients and their families.

Advanced surgical and imaging technologies allow more precise and more lung-preserving treatment

Today’s more advanced surgical technologies mean doctors can perform even complex lung cancer surgeries using minimally invasive techniques. In robotic-assisted thoracic surgery, robotic tools function like extensions of a surgeon’s hands, moving with precision to remove cancerous tissue. These tools can be used to remove tumors located deep in the lung that just five years ago would have required traditional open surgeries.

The latest imaging technologies allow doctors to pinpoint the precise location of the cancer, enabling removal of a smaller portion of the lung while preserving as much of the healthy lung as possible (segmentectomy). Until recently, patients were more likely to need a full lobe removed (lobectomy).

New immunotherapies and targeted treatments are changing what’s possible

Beyond surgery, treatment options for lung cancer are rapidly expanding for more advanced or harder-to-treat lung cancers. A new class of immunotherapy medications known as checkpoint inhibitors unleash a person’s own immune system to attack tumors. Another is targeted therapy, used when cancer cells carry certain genetic mutations. These mutations give faulty instructions to the cells, acting as signals to grow or spread. Using medications designed to precisely block those specific signals stops the lung cancer from growing or spreading. Combined with surgery, chemotherapy, or radiation, these therapies can extend survival and improve quality of life.

A simple scan can catch lung cancer early, when it’s curable

When detected in its early, most treatable stages, lung cancer can have a cure rate of over 90 percent. Early identification is usually possible only through screening because early-stage lung cancer rarely causes noticeable symptoms. Screenings are performed quickly and painlessly with a low-dose CT (computerized tomography) scan that takes less than two minutes and can spot nodules just a few millimeters in size — too small to be visible on an X-ray. Artificial Intelligence also now plays a growing role in screening, identifying subtle signs such as small lesions tucked near blood vessels or the delicate tissue lining the lungs. It can differentiate these from healthy cells, precisely measure the nodule size, and track growth, allowing for earlier and more accurate follow-up.

Ask your provider if you are eligible for lung cancer screening. The National Cancer Institute recommends lung cancer screening for people who:

Are 50 to 80 years old
Smoke currently or have quit in the past 15 years
Have a 20 “pack-year” smoking history, equivalent to smoking one pack a day for 20 years, two packs a day for 10 years, and so on.

Talk to your doctor if you have any lung cancer symptoms, which can include:

A persistent cough or one that continues to get worse
Shortness of breath or wheezing
Feeling tired all the time
Weight loss that happens for no apparent reason
Coughing up blood

What puts someone at higher risk for lung cancer?

Risk rises with age; most diagnoses occur at 65 or older. While 20 percent of people with lung cancer have never smoked, smoking remains the top cause. Other risks include secondhand smoke, prolonged radon or other toxic exposures (asbestos, arsenic), air pollution, prior chest radiation, and family history. Your doctor can review your individual risks and recommend screening or next steps.
