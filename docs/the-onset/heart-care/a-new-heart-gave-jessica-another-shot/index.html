A new heart gave Jessica another shot

By Dr. Daniel Goldstein, Vice Chairman of Cardiothoracic and Vascular Surgery at Montefiore Einstein, Heart Care · 6 min read

In short

Lucky. Thankful. Happy. These are the words 31-year-old Jessica Green uses to describe her life today, but it wasn't always that way. For as long as she can remember, Jessica had problems with her heart. At age 15, she was diagnosed with cardiomyopathy, a disease in which heart muscles weaken and have difficulty pumping blood. By 21, she had a pacemaker-defibrillator implanted to control her erratic heartbeat, but Jessica never let cardiac issues slow her down. Though she ran, played volleyball, and shot hoops with her friends, her ability to maintain an active lifestyle began to dwindle by her 27th birthday.

Saving Jessica with a bridge to a new heart: from pacemaker to LVAD to heart transplant

At first, the changes were subtle. She found herself more tired than usual by the end of the workday; after a while, stairs left her so winded that she couldn’t speak. Eventually, even walking short distances was almost impossible. “Just walking across the street would take me five minutes because I couldn’t breathe,” she says. “It felt like somebody was crushing my chest. That was the worst. Gasping for air was just horrible.”

Nighttime brought no relief. Unable to recline in bed without feeling like she was suffocating, the only way Jessica could get comfortable enough to sleep was by kneeling on her bedroom floor to rest her head on the foot of her bed, like a child saying her prayers at night. But what Jessica was praying for was her next breath.

After months of declining health, Jessica’s cousin insisted she go to the hospital. When she arrived at Montefiore Einstein, we discovered her damaged heart was only working at 5% capacity. Jessica had a drastic deterioration of her condition and quality of life, and her survival was measured in weeks to months at best. Her only choice was to be implanted with a left ventricular assist device (LVAD), a mechanical pump implanted inside the chest to help a weakened heart circulate five liters of blood per minute around the body. This machine is internal, like a pacemaker. Nobody sees it or hears it or feels it, not even the patient.

Though the pump is internal, a wire connects it to external batteries. Patients live what we call a tethered existence. They do always have to be tethered to a power source, either to batteries, which they carry in a purse that weighs about 8.5 pounds, or they have to be connected to an outlet. So it’s one of the major adjustments of living with an LVAD. The other limitation is the inability to submerge in water, though they can shower with some precautions.

The sudden news that she would require immediate surgery to save her life was almost more than Jessica could take. “I cried,” she says. “I was scared because I didn’t know about the machine. And you have batteries that you have to change, and a cord coming out of your stomach. It was nerve-racking.” We were able to soothe her fears, and the four-hour surgery was a success.

With an LVAD pumping sufficient blood to her body for the first time in years, Jessica’s life dramatically changed. No longer weak and winded, she was able to resume most of the activities she loves: going to concerts, working out at the gym, and traveling. Best of all, it meant she could finally sleep comfortably in her bed, rather than leaning on it.

Jessica was so grateful to the doctors at Montefiore-Einstein that she decided to give back. She started counseling patients who were being considered for LVADs, explaining that the device would change their lives for the better. When a surgeon or cardiologist walks into the room and talks to them about this complex titanium machine, it can be utterly overwhelming. Having a patient with the device telling them what life is like living with an LVAD is much more real. When Jessica showed up in patients’ hospital rooms, many failed to realize that this young and vibrant woman had an LVAD herself, until she showed them the wire keeping her tethered to her purse. Jessica told them, “Don’t give up. You can get back to normal. You’ll still be able to do everything you did before.”

The final steps to “better-than-ever” — now at age 33

Though Jessica resumed a reasonably normal life post-surgery, her LVAD was only a temporary solution. For something more permanent, she would need a heart transplant. Unfortunately, during her years of failing health and immobility, Jessica had gained so much weight that she was ineligible for the national heart transplant list. We know that morbid obesity is a problem for transplant patients because some of the medications they have to take to prevent rejection will make you heavier, and that’s going to affect your survival. So all transplant programs have a limit, a cutoff on what we call BMI, or body mass index. Above that cutoff we don’t offer transplants.

We suggested that Jessica consider bariatric surgery to reduce the size of her stomach and lose weight, but she wasn’t convinced. “I said, ‘No, no, no. I don’t need any more surgery. I’m going to just work it out on my own.’” But the pounds didn’t come off. Faced with the realization that she might never succeed in dropping the weight necessary to be considered for the transplant list, Jessica reluctantly had the procedure.

“When I finally did the surgery, it was like, ‘I should’ve done this ages ago,’” she says. Within months, Jessica lost more than 100 pounds and finally had her name added to the national heart transplant list. At last, on August 24, 2019, after four years of waiting, Jessica received word that a donor heart was finally available. During the four-hour surgery, doctors removed her old, damaged heart, along with the LVAD and her defibrillator, and transplanted one that would not require LVADs, pacemakers, or anything other than a balanced diet and exercise.

Today, Jessica feels better than ever. “I feel free. I feel less weight. I don’t have to carry the batteries. They were heavy,” Jessica says with a laugh. “I go to the gym. I work out to make sure my heart gets stronger.” And knowing that she was one of only 2,000 Americans to receive a heart transplant that year has left Jessica profoundly grateful. “I’m so lucky to get a second chance. I’m so thankful. I’m so happy. Thank you, Montefiore.”

Montefiore Einstein’s Center for Heart and Vascular care is ranked in the top 1% of all hospitals in the nation for cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery by U.S. News & World Report

Montefiore Einstein’s nationally recognized Center for Heart and Vascular Care has also achieved a “high performing” ranking in heart failure from U.S. News & World Report. It has one of the best heart transplant survival rates in New York and among the highest in the nation. It serves patients across the tri-state area, the U.S., and around the world, and is the only destination in New York for bloodless heart transplantation.

Following the opening of the Montefiore Einstein Cardiac Surgery Program at White Plains Hospital in November 2021, more than 100 hearts and lives have been saved. Montefiore Einstein’s Heart Failure, Heart Transplantation, and Mechanical Circulatory Support Programs are restoring lives through innovative heart care for our patients every day. Our unique patient care model provides comprehensive, individualized treatment, leveraging the vast resources available to our specialists, including groundbreaking proprietary research, and the latest state-of-the-art technologies for diagnosis and treatment.

When we developed the first transvenous cardiac pacemaker and performed the first coronary bypass operation, it was only the beginning of Montefiore Einstein’s more than 60 years of leadership in heart care — and we’re not slowing down.
