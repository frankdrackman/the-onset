Restore a normal heart rhythm. Cut stroke risk.

By Luigi Di Biase, MD, PhD, System Director of Electrophysiology · 5 min read · Restablezca un ritmo cardiaco normal y reduzca el riesgo de ACV

In short

With symptoms such as a racing, fluttering heartbeat and shortness of breath, atrial fibrillation (AFib) is more than uncomfortable — it makes stroke five times more likely and, over time, can weaken the heart and lead to heart failure. The encouraging news? Advances in treatment are changing the outlook dramatically. Pulsed field ablation (PFA) — a next-generation form of cardiac ablation — uses breakthrough technology to gently target the heart’s electrical system, restore a normal rhythm, and slow the progression of AFib. This minimally invasive procedure, developed with help from physicians at Montefiore Einstein, carries a lower risk of complications than traditional ablation. As a result, it’s now being recommended earlier in the course of AFib for many patients — offering a path to better rhythm control and lower stroke risk. Here’s how it works — and what you need to know.

How breakthroughs in ablation for AFib are beating irregular heartbeats

Why ablation is now being recommended earlier for more people

Ablation is a catheter-based treatment that treats heart tissue to disrupt the faulty electrical signals that cause arrhythmia. PFA uses electrical signals to target heart tissue with more precision, increasing its safety and effectiveness by reducing damage to nearby heart structures. Advanced imaging technologies are also improving the success of ablation procedures.

Previously, cardiologists recommended ablation as a treatment for AFib only if medications to control heart rhythm weren’t proving effective. Today, thanks to technological advancements with the ablation procedure, doctors recommend it as soon as possible even for people with persistent AFib, a form of AFib where arrhythmia episodes last longer than seven days. It’s also advised earlier for some patients with other types of the condition: paroxysmal AFib, where episodes start and stop quickly, lasting fewer than seven days, and persistent long-term AFib, where they continue for longer than one year. Like any other surgical procedure, ablation carries some risks, but they are rare when compared with the risk of living with severe AFib.

How AFib disrupts heart rhythm and raises stroke risk

The heart contains specialized cells that generate an electric signal that causes your heart to contract, pumping the blood that supplies your body with the oxygen and nutrients it needs. Your heart’s natural pacemaker, called the “sinus node,” generates this electrical signal that moves from the two chambers at the top of the heart (the atria) down to the two lower chambers, creating coordinated and orderly contractions. In AFib, there is random electrical activity in the heart that interrupts the normal rhythm, causing the atria to contract chaotically, which can leave blood pooling in the left atrium or the left atrial appendage, where it can clot. This is the reason for the increased risk of stroke with AFib, because a clot that makes its way to the brain can block an artery and cause a stroke. Aside from the stroke risk, AFib contributes to heart failure as it weakens the heart muscle, and it can cause disabling fatigue as the heart struggles to deliver oxygen and nutrients to the brain and body.

Who needs to watch for irregular heart rhythm?

AFib mostly occurs in people between 50 and 90 years old, though it is sometimes found in teens and young, active adults too. While research has not fully identified a cause of this condition, AFib is most often seen in people with a combination of health and lifestyle factors such as prior heart attack, obesity, diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), smoking, and/or excessive alcohol consumption, though it does sometimes show up in marathon runners and world-class athletes. For the patient with AFib, addressing comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, and diabetes can be helpful. About 70% of AFib electrical triggers are found in the pulmonary veins, which transfer oxygenated blood from the lungs to the heart. If you have these triggers and drink too much alcohol, smoke, or are obese, AFib may be triggered more frequently.

The goal of treatment is to prevent stroke, control the heart rate, and restore normal heart rhythm, slowing the progression of AFib. Identifying and managing treatable and reversible conditions such as obesity, OSA, hypertension, hypo- or hyperthyroidism, inflammatory and infectious diseases, and stress is also an important part of care for this condition.

Palpitations — a racing, fluttering, pounding, or irregular-feeling heartbeat — are the most common symptom of AFib, but between 20% and 25% of those with AFib have no symptoms.
