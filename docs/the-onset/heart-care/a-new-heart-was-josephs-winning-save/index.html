A new heart was Joseph's winning save

By Dr. Ulrich P. Jorde, Vice Chief of Cardiology and Head of Heart Failure at Montefiore Einstein, Heart Care · 6 min read · La jugada salvadora de Joseph fue un nuevo corazón

In short

Like most kids growing up in Costa Rica, Joseph Downes was obsessed with soccer. He spent every spare minute kicking that black and white ball toward the goal. “I really liked soccer a lot,” he says. “I used to play maybe two, three games a day.” But while his enthusiasm was equal to the other players, his stamina didn’t quite measure up. “I was always getting tired faster than the other kids,” he says. “As I was growing up, the problem was getting worse: shortness of breath, getting tired, feeling fatigued.” Doctors told Joseph’s mother there was nothing to worry about; he was just out of shape. But the constant exhaustion grew worse until finally, in his mid-20s, Joseph stopped playing entirely.

Joseph’s bloodless heart transplant preserved both life and conscience

The next few years of Joseph’s life went by unremarkably. By his early 30s, he was living in the United States and had a great job, a wonderful wife, and two gorgeous children. He rarely gave a second thought to how tired he felt most of the time, then one lazy day at home he couldn’t even muster the energy to climb the stairs to his bedroom. His breathing became labored, and when his wife noticed his entire body was swollen, she insisted he go to their local hospital in New Jersey.

“They told me, ‘Something doesn’t look good with your heart,’” Joseph remembers. “‘We’re going to start doing some tests.’” Tests revealed an arrhythmia and enlarged heart, surrounded by excessive amounts of fluid, that kept Joseph in the hospital for 22 days while doctors attempted to determine the cause. “At the end of the days that I was in the hospital, they said that my heart was not functioning like normal, but they couldn’t give me a reason for it.”

Once Joseph was stable, the hospital sent him home with medication and a “let’s see what happens” approach. After only a few weeks, his condition worsened and he had to be readmitted to the same New Jersey hospital. This time doctors were able to diagnose restrictive cardiomyopathy, a rare congenital heart condition in which the lower chambers of the heart become too rigid to expand as they fill with blood. Joseph says, “The doctor told me, ‘The condition that you have — it’s not treatable. We can’t do a surgery to fix it. The only thing that could correct that is a heart transplant.’”

Though terrified, Joseph was eager and willing to have a heart transplant if it would save his life. But there was one major issue. Due to his beliefs as a Jehovah’s Witness, Joseph couldn’t accept blood transfusions. “And the doctor told me, ‘Oh, that’s not going to be possible in this hospital. We don’t have a bloodless program, and it’s a risky surgery.’”

Joseph began calling hospitals, trying to find one willing to perform a heart transplant bloodlessly. One after another declined, saying they weren’t capable of this dangerous and complex surgery. Time passed and the denials stacked up as Joseph’s heart grew weaker, requiring hospitalization every few months. Unable to give him the heart transplant he desperately needed, doctors could only stabilize Joseph and then send him home.

Eventually, Joseph discovered two medical centers equipped to perform bloodless heart transplants — one in Chicago, the other in Houston. But in order to be added to the transplant lists in those cities, he would have to move there — a difficult proposition for a man with an infant, a 5-year-old, and a wife working full time to support the family. “I said to my wife, ‘If I want to live, I will have to move to Chicago.’ But the thought of moving there by myself was really difficult, and I started losing hope.”

That’s when Joseph’s doctor made a spectacular discovery at a cardiology convention. Joseph says, “My doctor told me, ‘There were doctors from a lot of hospitals, and bloodless heart transplants came up in the conversation. An internationally recognized expert in the management of congestive heart failure told me that they do bloodless heart transplants at his hospital, and he’s really interested in your case.’”

That’s when I met Joseph. After evaluating his records and performing some tests, I agreed to do what no other hospital within an 800-mile radius could: save Joseph’s life. “When he said he could do it,” says Joseph. “I looked at my wife and we both started crying. We couldn’t believe it.”

Joseph was added to the national heart transplant list in November 2016. Within a month, his health declined so rapidly that doctors made it clear he would never leave the hospital unless it was with a new heart.

With medicines keeping him temporarily stable, all Joseph could do now was wait. “It was really, really hard not being at home, taking care of my family,” Joseph says. “And especially because you don’t know how long you’re going to be there. It can be a month, it can be 3 months, it can be 8 months. You don’t know.”

Finally, on April 6, 2017, Joseph received word that his new heart was en route to Montefiore Einstein. “I was so happy,” says Joseph. “Finally, I’m going to be able to be with my kids again and play soccer. And obviously I was thankful to God, ’cause not a lot of people get a heart. A lot of people don’t make it to the surgery. That’s like a second chance at life.”

After the complex surgery, Joseph started feeling better almost immediately. “The change — it was amazing. I think that was the first time I’ve felt how having a good heart feels. My breathing, even my skin color, got back to normal. Before the surgery, I was pale, my hands were always cold. But the next day, my wife was sitting next to me, and she grabbed my hand and she’s like, ‘I have never felt your hands like that.’ Because they were warm now.”

Joseph left the hospital after only one week, where he finally had the energy to keep up with his two active children. Before long, he was even able to return to his first love, soccer. “I’m trying to play as much soccer as I can,” he says. “Now I’m able to play a full game — 90 minutes — without having to tell the coach to take me out because I can’t breathe.

“I have no words to say how grateful I am. I’m amazed by this hospital. I always tell people with heart problems, ‘Go to Montefiore-Einstein. Go over there if you can. They treat you with love.’”

Montefiore Einstein’s Center for Heart and Vascular care is ranked in the top 1% of all hospitals in the nation for cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery by U.S. News & World Report

Montefiore Einstein’s nationally recognized Center for Heart and Vascular Care has also achieved a “high performing” ranking in heart failure from U.S. News & World Report. It has one of the best heart transplant survival rates in New York and among the highest in the nation. It serves patients across the tri-state area, the U.S., and around the world, and is the only destination in New York for bloodless heart transplantation.

Following the opening of the Montefiore Einstein Cardiac Surgery Program at White Plains Hospital in November 2021, more than 100 hearts and lives have been saved. Montefiore Einstein’s Heart Failure, Heart Transplantation, and Mechanical Circulatory Support Programs are restoring lives through innovative heart care for our patients every day. Our unique patient care model provides comprehensive, individualized treatment, leveraging the vast resources available to our specialists, including groundbreaking proprietary research, and the latest state-of-the-art technologies for diagnosis and treatment.

When we developed the first transvenous cardiac pacemaker and performed the first coronary bypass operation, it was only the beginning of Montefiore Einstein’s more than 60 years of leadership in heart care — and we’re not slowing down. See more of Joseph’s story here.

Dr. Ulrich P. Jorde, Vice Chief of Cardiology and Head of Heart Failure at Montefiore Einstein
