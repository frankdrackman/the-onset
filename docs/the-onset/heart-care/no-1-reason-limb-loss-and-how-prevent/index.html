The No. 1 reason for limb loss and how to prevent it

By By: Stephanie Rand, DO, Associate Director rehabilitation Medicine Program, Montefiore Einstein · 3 min read

In short

If asked what it would feel like to live life without a limb, most people would have a difficult time imagining the experience. Yet, on average, 507 Americans lose a limb every day and about 2 million live with limb loss today, and that number is expected to nearly double by 2050. It’s a group whose stories include battles with disease, overcoming physical trauma, and mentally and physically coping with events that have changed life forever.

Most amputations result from vascular disease. Catching and treating it early is critical.

While 46% of amputations are caused by trauma, and a very small number are due to cancer, the majority — 54% — are the result of vascular disease. This is the most preventable cause of amputation. Here’s what you need to know about limb loss prevention.

Limb loss affects more than twice as many men as women, and more than 80% of amputees are over age 45. About two-thirds of amputations are lower limbs, and African Americans are four times more likely than white Americans to experience the loss of a limb.

In 54% of amputations, vascular disease, specifically peripheral artery disease (PAD), is the culprit. PAD is characterized by narrowed arteries that reduce blood flow to the extremities. This can cause something as trivial as a blister or small wound to fail to heal, setting in motion a sequence of events that too often results in the loss of a limb. In fact, according to the Amputee Coalition, about 85% of lower limb amputations are preceded by a foot ulcer. PAD is on the rise, yet it is both preventable and treatable.

Fortunately, advances in treatment and limb-sparing approaches have reduced limb loss.

Recognizing and managing vascular disease to prevent limb loss

PAD is primarily caused by diabetes, cigarette smoking and high blood pressure. Quitting smoking and managing chronic conditions is key to preventing PAD from progressing. Most people with vascular disease suffer from diabetes, which is also on the rise. Controlling both diabetes and high blood pressure with medication, diet and exercise can prevent PAD and limb loss.

If you have any of these symptoms, discuss them with your doctor immediately. Advances in vascular surgery have allowed many patients to avoid amputation, and treating the underlying conditions can slow progression of vascular disease.

One symptom of increased severity of vascular disease is a loss of sensation in your feet, and if you have PAD and/or diabetes, caring for your feet and legs is essential. You will need to check your feet and legs for sores and wounds daily. If a wound goes unrecognized and untreated due to this lack of sensation, there’s the potential for infection or gangrenous activity, which could increase your risk for amputation. If you see a blister or other changes to your feet, you should speak to your doctor.
