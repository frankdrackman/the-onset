Cardiac arrest, heart attack, or heart failure? What's the difference?

By Dr. Andrew K. Krumerman, Professor of Medicine, Heart Care · 5 min read · ¿Paro cardíaco, ataque al corazón o insuficiencia cardíaca? ¿Cuál es la diferencia?

In short

We hear the terms “heart attack,” “heart failure,” and “cardiac arrest” all the time, but what sets these conditions apart from each other — and how are they related? While they might sound like different ways to describe the same thing, each is unique. Knowing the difference, and what to do when faced with these commonly confused heart conditions, can help save lives.

Understanding the what and why of these three heart conditions

A heart attack (or myocardial infarction) is caused by a blockage in the arteries that feed into the heart muscle. When its blood supply is cut off or reduced, the heart muscle begins to suffer irreversible damage and is unable to pump efficiently. Immediate medical intervention is needed to clear a blocked artery and restore blood flow. Heart attacks can be “mild,” with few long-term effects, or more serious, leaving the heart unable to function at full capacity.

A person having a heart attack is usually conscious and able to describe their symptoms. These typically include chest pressure or pain, pain in the left arm or left jaw, sometimes associated with shortness of breath, sweating, or severe weakness. It’s important to know that symptoms may look different in women, who may also experience nausea, vomiting, and back pain. A person having a heart attack needs emergency medical services and a quick transfer to a hospital for further care. If you or someone you are with is experiencing symptoms of a heart attack, call 911 immediately.

Symptoms of cardiac arrest include dizziness, shortness of breath, and loss of consciousness.

Unlike a heart attack, which is caused by a blocked artery, heart failure is when the heart is unable to pump efficiently enough to meet the body’s needs. It can happen during a heart attack, but it’s often a chronic, long-term condition. Heart failure is most often caused by damage from a heart attack, coronary artery disease, chronic high blood pressure, cardiomyopathy (heart muscle disease), an irregular heartbeat, or drug or alcohol abuse. Because the heart isn’t pumping efficiently, the body’s organs may not be receiving the blood they need, which can cause complications such as kidney disease, liver damage, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and increased risk of stroke.

People with heart failure experience symptoms like fatigue, difficulty breathing, coughing, and swelling related to water retention. Treatment for heart failure can significantly improve heart muscle function. Medications are available to help increase the heart’s efficiency. Lifestyle changes can also reduce your risk. Some patients with heart failure are candidates for an implantable defibrillator, a small device, similar to a pacemaker, embedded in the chest that can deliver an electrical shock should you develop a cardiac arrest. See your doctor if you think you might be experiencing signs or symptoms of heart failure.

During cardiac arrest, the heart suddenly — and often without warning — stops beating. This happens when the heart’s electrical system, which controls its rhythm, is disrupted. People who go into cardiac arrest almost always have preexisting heart conditions, although they are not always aware of it. These include coronary artery disease, arrythmia, structural damage caused by a heart attack, and congenital issues (heart problems since birth).

Symptoms of cardiac arrest include dizziness, shortness of breath, and loss of consciousness. People in cardiac arrest will become unresponsive and stop breathing. They have no pulse, and vital organs such as the brain are no longer receiving blood and oxygen. They can die within minutes, leading to what’s known as sudden cardiac death. While waiting for EMTs to arrive, CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation) is the primary method to treat cardiac arrest and can double or triple a person’s chance of survival. The heart can be restarted with an automated external defibrillator (AED), a machine that bystanders or EMTs use upon arrival to “zap” the heart with an electrical current.

If you or someone you’re with experiences symptoms of cardiac arrest, call 911 immediately. In many public places, including schools and at professional sporting events, AEDs are available so people who are not EMTs can help save a life. Getting trained in CPR is another way you can be ready to help in case of an emergency.

Knowing the difference, and what to do, can save lives
