Culturally competent Alzheimer's care benefits patients and their caregivers

By Jessica Zwerling, M.D., Brain & Nerve Care · 4 min read

In short

During Alzheimer’s Awareness Month in June, we’re reminded of the importance of routine screening for early signs of Alzheimer’s disease. Screening can be more challenging in diverse communities where language and cultural differences make traditional screening tools less effective. Screening and testing in the patient’s native language, with familiar cultural references, enhances accuracy of the diagnosis and improves communication with family and caregivers.

The scope of the Alzheimer’s challenge is huge. Approximately 5.8 million U.S. residents are currently diagnosed, a number expected to double by 2050. Some 16 million unpaid caregivers will provide an estimated 18.5 billion hours of care for them in 2019. These caregivers face elevated stress levels and increased risks for heart attack, stroke and infection and cultural and language barriers can increase these risks. New approaches to physician training and multidisciplinary care for diverse populations are making inroads, improving both the process of diagnosis and supporting caregivers in their communities.

Family members play a crucial role in noticing the early changes that may point to Alzheimer’s disease. Common symptoms include pulling back from work or social activities, changes in mood or personality, challenges with solving problems or even preparing a favorite family recipe. If Mom or Dad forgets where they left the car keys, that’s not dementia. But if they’re unable to retrace their steps to find them it may be a signal, especially if there are other symptoms.

While Alzheimer’s is the most common form of dementia, accounting for 60%-80% of cases, it’s not the only condition that can cause loss of memory and other cognitive abilities that interfere with daily life. Early, accurate diagnosis is essential because while each form of dementia has symptoms similar to Alzheimer’s, each benefit from different patient- and culture-specific approaches. Though there is no cure for Alzheimer’s, there are many ways to empower patients and families which will provide more time for the patient, family and caregivers to prepare to manage the stages of the illness.

Alzheimer’s diagnosis begins with asking questions and getting background information from loved ones. In some cultures, Alzheimer’s is a taboo topic. Other groups are less accepting of treatment for depression. Some ethnic groups have elevated risk for diabetes and hypertension. In addition to looking for signs of dementia such as disorientation, language difficulty or behavioral disturbances, screening for depression and anxiety and other medical conditions that may impact brain function such as sleep apnea, substantial weight loss and hypoglycemia, or interactions of prescription drugs is essential. Many of these conditions can exist alongside dementia, or can cause a misdiagnosis, so culturally relevant tools and training help to accurately identify Alzheimer’s disease and understand the stage of progression.

For Alzheimer’s patients and families seeking culturally competent support and care, Montefiore is a recognized leader in the region. The Montefiore Hudson Valley Center of Excellence for Alzheimer's Disease (CEAD) is one of 10 Alzheimer’s Disease Centers of Excellence supported in part by a grant from the New York State Department of Health in an ambitious program to expand knowledge about Alzheimer’s Disease and related dementias. The Montefiore CEAD is improving access to screening, diagnosis and clinical trial opportunities for patients; connecting patients and their caregivers to community-based support services; and training physicians and allied health professionals in all clinical disciplines to work effectively with diverse patient populations.

Montefiore currently has the capacity to test patients for Alzheimer’s and memory loss in 17 different languages and provide face-to-face interpreters. We’ve developed culture-fair screening tools that remove literacy barriers by using pictures, so patients are asked to name the item in the image and remember it later, rather than using words.

Montefiore's multidisciplinary teams include specialists in neurology, neuropsychology, geriatrics, and geriatric psychiatry, along with social workers and care coordinators who provide both patient and caregiver with the support they need to navigate through culturally relevant resources. Just as we connect patients with community-based services where they live, we work with community organizations to raise awareness of our programs within those communities. Montefiore engages at the grass roots level, working through religious organizations, community groups and government agencies that serve diverse communities.

Although there is no cure for Alzheimer’s, Montefiore works to improve the quality of life of every patient and caregiver, making the most of every day.

The Montefiore Hudson Valley Center of Excellence for Alzheimer’s Disease (CEAD) provides culture competent collaborative care for cognitively impaired individuals - the 5 critical Cs - from the Hudson River Valley and tri-state area, offering a unique approach to treatment that helps patients with cognitive issues as well as their families. The multidisciplinary center works with people from diverse cultural backgrounds to develop comprehensive, coordinated treatment plans. CEAD combines the years of expertise and best-in-class practices of the Montefiore Einstein Center for the Aging Brain (CAB); Burke Rehabilitation Hospital’s Memory Evaluation and Treatment Service (METS); an expanding network of hospitals and physician groups in the Hudson Valley; and the research and training capabilities of Montefiore’s Albert Einstein College of Medicine.
