Even three brain tumors couldn't upstage Barbara and steal her second act

By Montefiore-Einstein, Brain & Nerve Care · 5 min read

In short

Barbara Higgins is a doer. A retired health educator and track coach, she says, “I like to challenge myself … I like to do things that people think I can’t do.” That love of a challenge was put to the ultimate test for this mother of two talented daughters, who was living a busy and fulfilling life in Concord, New Hampshire. This is how she came to be living her second act, after overcoming challenges that could have brought the curtain down.

Barbara’s daughters, Gracie and Molly, were as close as twins despite their two-year age difference. Both girls were accomplished singers and dancers who frequently performed together, while Barbara was their supermom — expertly managing lesson and rehearsal schedules, costume fittings, and all the other activities of a busy family.

Life took a tragic and shocking turn in the spring of 2016, when Molly began to suffer brutal headaches. Despite multiple trips to the doctor, Molly’s condition deteriorated. One last time, Barbara took Molly to the ER where the 13-year old lost consciousness and could not be revived. A massive, undiagnosed brain tumor took her life within a few days. Heartbroken, Barbara staged an uplifting tribute to Molly two weeks later at Concord’s Capitol Center, performing with Gracie and her daughters’ beloved Concord Dance Academy in front of a packed audience who shared their burden of grief.

No break from the pain: Barbara needs a neurosurgeon

Now Barbara’s challenge was to help Gracie, then 15, survive this profound loss, while mourning Molly, and helping her husband Kenny cope with the loss and his worsening health. Barbara found herself needing to power through constant pain in her face from a preexisting chronic nerve condition called trigeminal neuralgia. “I pretty much hurt all the time,” says Barbara. “Think of the worst toothache you've ever had, and it was like that all the time. Sometimes it was a knifing pain, and then every once in awhile I would get like a surge of pain that would bring me to my knees. It was like being electrocuted almost. It was really, really, alarmingly painful and scary.”

Understanding her reluctance to be treated at the local hospital, a coworker encouraged Barbara to see Dr. Emad Eskandar, despite the four-and-a-half hour drive it would require. Known around the world as an expert in trigeminal neuralgia, Dr. Eskandar is Chair of the Montefiore-Einstein Department of Neurological Surgery. Barbara made an appointment, and started on a journey that would lead to her next enormous challenge.

Upon meeting Barbara, Dr. Eskandar immediately ordered an MRI to pinpoint the source of her facial pain. What appeared on the MRI was completely unexpected: three brain tumors, including one the size of a golf ball. Barbara’s reaction was, “I just thought, why me? Like I haven’t had enough already?” Molly had just died of a brain tumor, her husband was in need of a kidney transplant, and Gracie was having trouble coping with all of the tragedy in their lives. “I said to Dr. Eskandar, ‘I just need you to fix this as fast as possible,’” Barbara says. “And, he took my hands in his hands, and he said, ‘you just tell Gracie that we'll take good care of you, and that you're going to be fine.’"

Dr. Eskandar and his multidisciplinary team performed two operations on Barbara — one to fix her nerve pain, and another to remove her brain tumors. “Luckily, that went extremely well, and she recovered beautifully,” Dr. Eskandar says. “A big part of neurosurgery is the planning. I tell people it’s 90% planning and 10% execution.” The night before a major surgery, Dr. Eskandar always sets aside time to review the patient’s chart and imaging results, as well as visualize the entire operation in his head to establish a plan of attack and make sure he has everything ready. “In essence, there should be no surprises during surgery,” Dr. Eskandar says.

“I will never have any serious medical work done anywhere but Montefiore,” Barbara says. “It's the most amazing place. I just feel so lucky, like the universe somehow sent me there.” Barbara was especially impressed with Dr. Eskandar’s compassionate care. Not only did he take the time to answer all her questions, he gave her his cell phone number in case she had any additional concerns. “It's a level of care that I'm not familiar with at all,” says Barbara. “I don't have any other doctors in my entire life that say, ‘Give me your phone,’ and put their cellphone number in there.” Dr. Eskandar responds, “I’ll often give patients my cellphone number. It really reduces the anxiety that patients sometimes have, knowing that if the need arose, I’m a mere text or phone call away.”

Today, Barbara is enjoying her second act. With her nerve pain gone, she is now officially tumor-free and she hopes sharing her story may help others. “I can't change it. I can't un-brain tumor my head. I can't go back and save Molly. So I can let it wreck me, and I can walk around mad and angry, or I can use my experience to help somebody else. So that maybe somebody will see this, and something will click in their head that they should make an appointment with Dr. Eskandar, or go to the cancer center, or whatever; that something in my story will resonate with someone else, and that will help them to fix what's broken inside of them.”
