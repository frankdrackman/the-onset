Why pediatric sports injuries are on the rise — and what to do about

By Dr. Eric Fornari, Co-Director, Orthopedics & Rehab · 4 min read

In short

As kids return to school and sports, it’s not surprising that injuries like stress fractures, tendinitis and muscle strains are on the rise, especially in kids. Kids have spent much of the past year and a half social distancing, doing PE classes over Zoom and sitting on the sidelines as many youth sports were put on hold for months. As is true for adults, moving from zero to 100 without acknowledging months of downtime can be a recipe for injury.

Kids are excited to get back to the sports they love and move forward in the pandemic. But in their rush to play, kids sometimes downplay small injuries that can lead to bigger problems. They often don’t understand that pushing through pain can lead to problems, like when a mildly injured joint collapses under more strenuous use, or when overworking a muscle leads to cartilage tears.

And they don’t always comprehend the effect this forced break may have had on their bodies in terms of deconditioning, weight gain and muscle tightness, all of which can lead to injuries around the shoulders, hips, knees and ankles. There is also a psychological component: Kids who have been isolated might be dealing with some anxiety, fears or depression as they return to the field. So it is important for young athletes, parents and coaches to set proper expectations and ease kids back into the sports they love.

The most important part of that transition is conditioning. Kids should engage in proper strength programs — warm-up and cool-down routines and stretching — before and after playing to prevent injuries. Conditioning programs implemented before the start of a season are known to decrease the number of ACL tears a soccer team will see throughout the course of that year. And as they return to sports, proper nutrition can help prevent stress injuries and fractures. Healthy eating also helps young athletes preserve lean tissue, manage weight and generally maintain healthy bodies.

No matter the sport or activity, every kid needs to build a strong core with exercises such a scissor kicks, crunches and hip lifts to develop good balance and strength throughout the body. It’s important for them to regain the stamina they may have lost so they can participate fully for the entirety of the season. Weight training — on machines or with free weights — resistance bands, push-ups and crab walking can be great ways for kids to improve their overall fitness and help prevent injuries. Of course, children should be properly supervised to ensure they are using any equipment correctly.

Once school and practice begin, parents should be on the lookout for red flags — whether their child is a varsity athlete or simply enjoys a round of recreational tennis. It’s not uncommon for kids to have growing pains, but when pain starts to interfere with routine activities, or there’s swelling, an inability to move a joint or the feeling that a joint is “stuck,” those are all signs that the injury needs further evaluation.

If a guardian or coach is concerned but not sure where to begin, they can check in with a pediatric orthopedist to better understand how to distinguish between different types of aches and pains, concerning signs and symptoms to look out for and how to establish the importance of cross-training, using different muscles at different times and taking rest days. A doctor’s expertise is particularly valuable here because there are unique aspects of the pediatric anatomy that are imperative to growth and development. By understanding these and other pediatric distinctions, doctors and guardians can work together to help kids obtain the best possible outcomes and prevent future injuries.

It’s important to be aware that an injury can cause psychological as well as physical issues. Lots of kids are suffering from being isolated during the pandemic. An injury can sideline them again for weeks, months or even a year. That break, coming just as they’re trying to resume their usual activities, can leave them feeling alone, scared, anxious or depressed.

Pediatric orthopedists can help patients work through these psychosocial issues. Sometimes, that’s 80% of our job. A patient’s mental state can have a profound impact on their recovery and often determines how well the patient responds to a specific orthopedic treatment or how much physical therapy they participate in.

Getting kids engaged in their treatment and healing requires doctors, coaches and parents to know the right questions to ask about how the injury is impacting interactions with friends and teammates and how students are navigating school — for example, asking whether there’s an elevator for them to use and strategizing solutions if not.

Internationally renowned Pediatric Orthopedics at Montefiore-Einstein

The Division of Pediatric Orthopedics at the Children’s Hospital at Montefiore (CHAM) provides care for patients with the full spectrum of musculoskeletal conditions. We continue to garner national and international attention for our innovative research and leading-edge approaches to treating deformities of the limbs and growing spines. Physicians within the Division of Pediatric Orthopedics at CHAM are experts in the full range of advanced treatment solutions, including Pediatric and Adolescent Sports Medicine.

We put extensive resources behind researching, diagnosing, treating and managing your child’s care. Specialists in physical therapy, pediatric rehabilitation medicine, orthotic and prosthetic services and other specialties within CHAM complement the expertise provided by our orthopedics team.

At Montefiore-Einstein, we’re working to develop formal relationships with high schools in the region to provide a comprehensive program that includes educational seminars, talks for athletes and their parents and outreach to athletic trainers.

We have expanded our Pediatric Orthopedic service in Westchester through our partnership with White Plains Hospital, a member of the Montefiore Health System, so that families in the region can easily access care closer to home.
