Kids get arthritis too. Know the signs.

By Dawn M. Wahezi, MD, Chief of Pediatric Rheumatology at the Children’s Hospital at Montefiore · 3 min read

In short

It’s normal for children to have growing pains as they get older, but believe it or not, kids get arthritis too. About 1 in every 1,000 U.S. kids under the age of 16 is diagnosed with what’s known as juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) every year. But JIA is not like the common osteoarthritis experienced by adults with aging joints. JIA — formerly known as juvenile rheumatoid arthritis — is actually an autoimmune condition, not something caused by normal joint wear and tear.

Juvenile arthritis: What parents need to know

The good news is that when JIA is diagnosed and treated early, children can go into remission, play sports, and otherwise lead full, active lives. But if left untreated, it can interfere with a child’s growth, damage their joints, and even cause vision problems. Here’s what parents need to know about this painful inflammatory condition.

Juvenile arthritis is often overlooked because kids are resilient

Though JIA is the most common rheumatic disease in children under 16, it’s often overlooked outside of pediatric rheumatology practices because kids are just incredibly resilient. JIA causes joint pain, so children will find ways to avoid painful activities like running or playing on playground equipment. They might not consider the pain significant enough to complain about. And younger children might not be able to articulate their discomfort.

When a child shows signs of joint pain, a specialist in pediatric rheumatology can rule out other conditions, diagnose if it’s JIA, and guide parents to the appropriate treatment.

There have been tremendous advancements in juvenile arthritis treatments over the past few decades. Specialists treat the disease with several options, from non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), like ibuprofen, to disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs).

What causes juvenile arthritis — and when are kids most at risk?

We don’t know exactly what causes JIA, but certain factors can put a child at higher risk of a diagnosis, including a genetic predisposition, exposure to smoking, and an immune system reaction to a virus. When the immune system malfunctions, it attacks different parts of the body, and with JIA this can mean inflammation of the joints, spine, skin, and eyes.

JIA is most commonly discovered when kids are either 1 to 3 years or 8 to 10 years old. Girls are diagnosed with JIA more often than boys, especially between ages 8 and 10 — likely because of the hormonal changes that occur during puberty.
