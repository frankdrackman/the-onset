RSV on the rise year-round: protecting babies and toddlers in warmer months

By Alyssa Silver, Attending Physician, Primary Care · 3 min read

In short

Despite it being summer, RSV is still active due to the COVID pandemic. Respiratory Syncytial Virus, most commonly known as RSV, affects babies and toddlers, especially infants under six months, who account for almost half of all cases. In its mildest form, RSV resembles the common cold. It is traditionally most prevalent from October through March. Though now the virus is affecting children all year round. The cause, while still unknown, may be because of precautions and changes in our behavior as well as viral interference, an interaction between SARS CoV-2 and other viruses.

RSV presents a significant health challenge to infants and very young children when it causes bronchiolitis, an infection of the smaller airways in the lungs. Bronchiolitis causes these airways, the bronchioles, to swell and become congested with mucus the body produces to fight off the virus. Because babies’ airways are so small, the impact can be much more serious than it is in an older child or adult. Other viruses, including SARS-CoV-2 (severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2), the virus that causes COVID-19, can also cause bronchiolitis, but RSV is the cause in 80% of cases in infants and accounts for most hospitalizations. Understanding how to recognize RSV and bronchiolitis symptoms, deal with the illness and reduce the risks of viral respiratory infection is important for parents.

What to watch for and when to seek medical attention

Virtually all children get an RSV infection by the time they are 2 years old. A stuffy nose is usually the first sign, with congestion that progresses down into the lungs, triggering a cough.

The virus is easily spread by respiratory droplets expelled when an infected person coughs or sneezes.

If the congestion cannot be brought under control, then faster and more difficult breathing follows. Symptoms are usually the worst between the third and fifth day of the illness. Certain children are more vulnerable to severe RSV infections and complications, including premature infants, young children with congenital heart or lung disease, children with compromised immune systems and infants in crowded childcare settings.

RSV enters the body through the eyes, nose or mouth. The virus is easily spread by respiratory droplets expelled when an infected person coughs or sneezes. RSV can live for hours on hard surfaces such as crib rails, countertops and toys. Touch your nose, eyes or mouth after touching a contaminated surface and you can pick up the virus. Unfortunately, there is no vaccine or medication for RSV or any of the viruses that cause bronchiolitis, with the sole exception of influenza, for which there is a vaccine and medicine that may shorten the course of the illness.
