Restoring function and improving quality of life for children with

By Oren Tepper, M.D., Primary Care · 4 min read

In short

The most common birth defects are craniofacial disorders, a term that covers conditions affecting the skull and facial bones, the soft tissue covering them, and the brain. Thanks to advances in treatment and technology, multidisciplinary teams of surgeons and specialists are able to improve outcomes of patients with craniofacial disorders from the fairly common to the extraordinarily complex.

The craniofacial birth defects we see most often

The three categories of birth defects seen most are cleft lip and cleft palate, craniosynostosis, and microtia.

Restoring function and appearance for cleft lip and/or palate

Treatment for craniosynostosis involves both neurosurgery and plastic surgery to remove part of the abnormal skull bone and then reconstruct so it can grow normally.

The first step in treating cleft lip is often “presurgical molding,” or nasoalveolar molding (NAM). A shaping device, similar to an aligner used to adjust teeth, is fitted to the infant almost immediately after birth. This is adjusted weekly to mold the tissue. At around three months, surgery will correct the cleft lip, while a cleft palate operation takes place at around one year. Infants with these disorders may have trouble feeding, so the care team will ensure that a specially designed nipple is available for the baby’s bottle. Care continues with an evaluation by speech therapy specialists at age 5 or 6, to determine if surgery is needed to realign the muscles used in talking. When patients reach their teens, more surgeries may be required to reestablish normal spacing between the upper and lower teeth, or more surgery to refine the nose.

Advanced technology and materials transform craniosynostosis treatment

Treatment for craniosynostosis involves both neurosurgery and plastic surgery to remove part of the abnormal skull bone and then reconstruct so it can grow normally. The development of resorbable plates for this purpose has transformed treatment for this disorder. Historically, titanium metal plates and screws were used, but the challenge with children is they’re growing, so you want plates to be there long enough to let them heal, but not forever. Resorbable plates are made from a material that can hold the bones in place for about nine to 12 months, long enough to heal, and then safely dissolve. Another advancement in craniosynostosis treatment is the use of 3D modeling and printing to provide a precise guide for the surgeons. With CT scans of the child’s skull, custom software allows the craniofacial team to create a 3D physical model and use this as a reference to help achieve the desired head shape.

To repair microtia, a new ear is often sculpted using the patient’s own tissue. This is done at around 8 or 10 years of age. At that point, the child has enough cartilage in their ribs that can be used to create the new ear, and that’s also the age when they have close to adult-sized ears. A process of three to four surgeries over the course of a year, microtia treatment begins by sculpting the foundation of the ear with rib cartilage and placing it into the head’s ear pocket. Over the next few surgeries, the lobe will be created and contours added to match the other ear as close as possible. In cases where there is only a mild deformity, such as prominent ears, a process of ear molding may be an option. In that case, the baby’s ear is molded over the four to six weeks following birth, when collagen is still very malleable, to avoid any need for later surgeries.

Montefiore's pioneering care, personalized for each child and family

The Craniofacial Center at the Children’s Hospital at Montefiore was one of the nation’s first and continues to lead advancements in craniofacial care. The center recently gained worldwide recognition for successfully separating twin boys, Anais and Jadon McDonald, who were born cranially conjoined with their brains intertwined.

Montefiore is one of only a few centers in the nation with a 3D printing lab, establishing a new standard of care for craniofacial surgeries. Craniofacial specialist teams at Montefiore include neurosurgeons; plastic surgeons; ear, nose, and throat specialists; dental and orthodontic specialists; child-life specialists; speech therapists; and expert nurse practitioners. Montefiore's multidisciplinary teams create personalized treatment plans for each patient, collaborating with outside experts and leveraging the most up-to-date research to innovate and provide the best outcomes for children and families who come to us from our region, across the country, and around the world.

As one of the country's first and leading craniofacial centers, the Craniofacial Center at the Children’s Hospital at Montefiore offers patients access to internationally recognized physicians and our highly skilled multidisciplinary team, a range of leading-edge advancements in craniofacial care and information to put both parents and children at ease. The Craniofacial Center’s team of specialists is among the most recognized and respected in the world. They’ve helped thousands of children born with birth anomalies, complex craniofacial tumors and post-traumatic craniofacial deformities.
