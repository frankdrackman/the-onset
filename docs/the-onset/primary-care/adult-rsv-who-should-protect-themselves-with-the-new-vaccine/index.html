Adult RSV: Who should protect themselves with the new vaccine?

By Gregory D. Weston, MD, Attending Physician–Infectious Diseases · 2 min read

In short

RSV, or respiratory syncytial virus, is a common cause of respiratory-tract infections. Typical symptoms of RSV resemble those of the common cold, including runny nose, sneezing, coughing, fever, wheezing, and decreased appetite, and they usually disappear within a couple of weeks. However, for certain adults, as with children under 5, it can seriously affect the lungs. Adults with underlying health conditions and those 60 years and over are more susceptible to RSV complications like asthma attacks, COPD exacerbation, pneumonia, and bronchiolitis, and people with congestive heart failure are at risk of more severe cardiac symptoms. Today, though, there’s a new way for adults and those who are pregnant to protect themselves from RSV.

RSV can hit adults hard: How you can hit back

Otherwise, if you’re not at higher risk, are not pregnant, and your symptoms are mild, there’s likely no reason to see a doctor. Treatment for mild RSV and the common cold is the same: rest, fluids, and over-the-counter pain relievers as needed. You can also protect others by staying at home and being careful to avoid anyone who might be at high risk of severe RSV. Each year, up to 160,000 Americans ages 65 and older are hospitalized due to RSV, and as many as 10,000 die from the illness and its complications.
