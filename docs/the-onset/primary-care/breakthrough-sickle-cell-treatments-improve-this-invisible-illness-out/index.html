Breakthrough sickle cell treatments improve this invisible illness outlook

By Kerry A. Morrone, MD, Director of the Sickle Cell Program for the Children’s Hospital at Montefiore · 3 min read

In short

Sickle cell disease is sometimes called an invisible illness, since, as a blood disorder, its symptoms are not always seen on the outside. Pain, vision loss, and even a stroke may be their first symptoms. While sickle cell was once considered fatal, new treatments and advances are helping people live longer than ever before, with dramatically improved quality of life.

Improving the lives of people with sickle cell disease

One of the most promising experimental treatments being studied as a potential cure for the disease is gene therapy, in which the gene that causes sickle cell disease is extracted, corrected, and then administered back to the patient. Other very promising clinical trials include a partial-match bone-marrow transplantation, which does not require a complete match with a donor. It uses healthy, blood-forming cells from a donor to replace unhealthy sickle cells. The donor is typically a family member but can also be an altruistic donor.

Significantly improving lives with advances in screening and treatment

Early diagnosis and treatment of sickle cell disease is crucial for slowing the disease’s progression and minimizing symptoms. Today, advanced screening methods allow diagnosis and treatment much earlier than before. U.S. hospitals now screen all newborns for sickle cell disease with a heel-stick blood test. All babies with the disease are prescribed an antibiotic that helps prevent dangerous infections. Young patients will also be prescribed folic acid and sometimes vitamin D to make up for deficiencies that often accompany sickle cell disease.

New treatment advances for teens and adults can help minimize the symptoms and complications. Anti-inflammatories and new oral and intravenous medications improve quality of life for people with sickle cell by reducing the number of painful crises associated with the disease; improving hemoglobin function, which delivers oxygen throughout the body; and decreasing instances of acute chest syndrome, an emergency complication that can include intense pain, cough, fever, low oxygen levels, and fluid buildup in the lungs. These screenings and treatments are also helping extend the lives of people with the disease.

What is sickle cell disease — and who is affected by it?

Sickle cell disease is caused by a genetic mutation that alters the shape of red blood cells, giving them a C-shape, like a sickle. The misshapen cells are not as effective at carrying oxygen, are fewer in number, and can cause blockages.

While sickle cell predominantly affects people of African descent, the truth is that people from many ethnic backgrounds — including those from the Middle East, India, Greece, Italy, Central America, and South America — are at risk of sickle cell disease. Since sickle cell disease is the result of an inherited genetic mutation, it’s not contagious.

People from other parts of the world may not have been screened at birth. Because it’s possible for sickle cell symptoms to be mistaken for other illnesses, anyone with symptoms who was born abroad and now lives in the U.S. can talk to their doctor about having a blood test for sickle cell disease.

Montefiore Einstein leads the way in the research and treatment of sickle cell disease in children and adults

U.S. News & World Report ranks Children’s Hospital at Montefiore (CHAM) one of the best in the nation for the 16th year in a row. Montefiore Einstein successfully collaborated with the New York State Department of Health to change the guidelines, making sickle cell disease a single qualifying condition for Health Home enrollment. This improvement creates an easier way for individuals with this condition to access care and coordinating services.

We are actively pursuing new agents to cure sickle cell disease, including gene therapy and partial-match bone-marrow transplantation, as well as new approaches to treat the effects of this disease, with the ultimate goal of finding new cures. Our patients have the opportunity to be actively enrolled in federally funded national clinical trials to explore new treatment methods.

At our Montefiore Einstein Sickle Cell Center for Adults (SCCA), physicians, nurse practitioners, a social worker, and patient advocate follow our adult patients in the outpatient clinic, in the emergency department, and on inpatient units, managing the medical and social service needs of patients with sickle cell disease. Montefiore Einstein was one of the first contributors to epigenetics, the study of how genetic factors impact inherited diseases like sickle cell. We continue to investigate sickle cell disease through genetic mapping, microvascular studies, and research.

Kerry A. Morrone, MD

Director of the Sickle Cell Program for the Children’s Hospital at Montefiore, Montefiore Einstein · 1 piece on The Onset
