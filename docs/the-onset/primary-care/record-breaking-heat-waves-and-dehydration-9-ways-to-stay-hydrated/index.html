Record-breaking heat waves and dehydration — 9 ways to stay hydrated

By Maria Dela Cruz, MD, Internal Medicine Specialist · 5 min read

In short

Record-breaking heat brings a greater potential for dehydration. We all hear that we should get 8 glasses of water a day, but is it enough for everyone to avoid dehydration? It’s a good rule of thumb for proper hydration to drink eight 8-ounce glasses of water per day. However, that may not be enough depending on your individual lifestyle, level of physical activity, the outdoor temperature, and the amount of caffeinated drinks you consume. In general, you need to replace more water than you lose, which can be more than eight 8-ounce glasses, or 64 ounces, of water per day.

The scoop on dehydration and how much water you really need

Staying well hydrated is not only essential to good health — including improving brain performance — new research also shows that it leads to healthier aging and fewer chronic diseases. Because more than half of our bodies are made up of water, getting plenty every day is essential, which is why dehydration can have such an impact on our health. When our bodies use more water than we take in, which is more likely on hot, humid summer days, we get dehydrated. Mood, memory, and reaction time are affected, and our kidneys, liver, and bowels struggle to function properly. By the time warning signs like thirst, fatigue, and dry skin appear, our organs may already be compromised. Feeling thirsty means that we are dehydrated and not getting enough water at baseline. Children, the elderly, and those with compromised immune systems or chronic health conditions are at higher risk of dehydration. Here are 9 ways to make sure you’re consuming enough water — and how to know if you’re replacing what you’re losing depending on how active you are, your caffeine intake, and the weather.

1. The more active you are, the more water you need. The more active you are, the more you sweat, making you more prone to dehydration. It’s important to replenish any fluids your body is losing by drinking extra water before, during, and after physical activity. Staying hydrated helps maintain energy levels and can also ensure, and even boost, function and performance.

What can happen when you don’t get as much water as you need

Carl LaRiviere, an amateur basketball player, learned the hard way that he wasn’t drinking as much water as he was losing.

2. Keep track of your water intake. In general, men need 3 liters (about 100 ounces) and women need 2.2 liters (about 75 ounces) daily. Use water bottles with measurements as opposed to glasses to keep track of how much you’re drinking. A 1-liter or 32-ounce sports bottle provides four of your daily eight glasses of water. Drink two bottles and you’ll be off to a great start. Some water bottles will alert you to pause for a drink every couple of hours, but you can always set alarms on your phone as well. Using a bottle with measurements is much more accurate than a “glass,” which comes in many sizes.

3. Watch out for caffeine and alcohol. Not all beverages are created equal when it comes to hydration. Coffee, alcoholic beverages, and some varieties of tea are diuretics that encourage your body to eliminate water though urine. For every ounce of these you drink, you need to drink an equal amount of water.

4. Eat water-rich fruits and vegetables. Watermelon, cantaloupe, grapes, and strawberries, along with veggies like cucumbers and celery, are all water-rich foods that help your body rehydrate. They’re also good hydrating snacks for active kids.

5. Drink water at mealtime. A glass of water 30 minutes before and one hour after a meal aids digestion. If you eat three times a day and follow this guideline, you’ll have polished off six of your eight glasses of water just with your meals.

6. Avoid prolonged sun exposure. After 30 minutes of sun, it’s time to find some shade. Your body cools itself by perspiring, which can accelerate dehydration, and if you’re working or playing hard, you’ll want to compensate by drinking more water.

7. Flavor your water. If you find water a little boring, add some flavor to jazz it up and inspire you to drink more. Try adding citrus fruits, berries, mint, or cucumber, or keep a pitcher of decaf, non-diuretic herbal tea like chamomile or hibiscus in the refrigerator. Seltzer is a good option if you like bubbles, but avoid drinks with additives and sugar.

8. Don’t rely on sports drinks for hydration. The average person doesn’t need the glucose- and electrolyte-enhanced sports drinks designed for athletes engaged in intense activity. They’re high in sugar and sodium. Just drinking water and eating a healthy diet with plenty of fruits and vegetables is the best way to stay hydrated.

9. Start every day with a glass of water. Before you take that first sip of coffee, drink an eight-ounce glass of water. Your body is using water all night while you’re sleeping, so you’ll feel better if you rehydrate first thing in the morning.

Babies and younger children are especially vulnerable to dehydration. The primary sign in an infant is a dry diaper, and they may be listless and have sunken eyes. Children ages 2 to 5 may be whiny and complain of belly pain, have crusty lips, and stop urinating. Common symptoms in adults include fatigue, dizziness, irregular or fast heartbeat, dry skin, chapped lips, and constipation.

Check the color of urine: It should be very pale yellow when you’re properly hydrated. When it starts looking dark yellow or brown, it’s time to drink more water. Another self-check for dehydration is to pinch and lift the skin on the back of your hand, like a tent. If it does not snap back right away, you need to drink more water.

If someone feels disoriented, is slurring their speech, and feels lightheaded, their condition is likely extreme and they’ll need IV fluids administered at a health care facility. Babies who do not urinate regularly, become less interactive, and have sunken eyes and cracked lips may also require IV fluids. Seek immediate medical attention.

The elderly are particularly at risk for dehydration because they may forget to drink enough water and may not notice when they’re thirsty. The symptoms of dehydration can mimic dementia or resemble a stroke in seniors. At this point, the person should be taken to the hospital immediately.

People with chronic health conditions need to be especially conscientious about hydration. For those with diabetes, proper nutrition and water intake are key to blood sugar control. Individuals with heart disease, cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, organ transplant recipients, and others who are immune-compromised may be taking medications with diuretic properties that could affect their hydration needs. Ask a doctor for hydration guidance.
