Hot humid weather raises risk of dehydration: Here's how to avoid it

By Dr. Sybil Hodgson, for Montefiore, Primary Care · 4 min read · La humedad y el calor aumentan el riesgo de deshidratación. Aquí encontrará algunos consejos para evitarla

In short

Over half of the human body is made up of water, therefore, consuming an adequate amount of water daily is essential for life. Age and gender largely influence the amount of water in your body. When your body uses more water than you take in, dehydration results, causing organs like your kidneys, liver and bowels to struggle to function properly. By the time physical warning signs like fatigue and dry skin appear, organs may already be compromised.

Hot, humid summer days increase the likelihood of dehydration, and working outdoors, playing sports, hanging out at the beach or even an afternoon barbecue, can bring on the condition if you go too long without drinking water. Babies, young children, the elderly and those with compromised immune systems or chronic health conditions are at higher risk.

Drinking water is the best way to stay hydrated all year long, and especially during the heat of summer. In general, an adult male needs about 3 liters of water per day while an adult female needs about 2.2 liters per day. A well-known rule of thumb is eight 8-ounce glasses per day, so that's a great starting point. Here are seven tips to help you hydrate:

1. Keep track of your water intake. A 32-ounce sports bottle provides four of your eight glasses of water. Drink two bottles and you are at a great starting point.

Your body is using water all night while you’re sleeping, so it makes sense that you need to hydrate first thing to get your day started.

2. Watch out for caffeine. Not all beverages are created equal when it comes to hydration. Coffee, alcoholic beverages and some varieties of tea are diuretics that encourage your body to eliminate water though urine. For every ounce of these you drink, you need to drink an equal amount of water. Keep alcohol consumption to a minimum, especially on hot days.

3. Avoid prolonged sun exposure. After 30 minutes, it’s time to find some shade. Your body cools itself by perspiring, which can accelerate dehydration, and if you’re working or playing hard, you’ll need to compensate by drinking more water.

4. Rely on a balanced diet for electrolytes. The average person doesn’t require the glucose- and electrolyte-enhanced sports drinks designed for athletes engaged in intense activity. These drinks are high in sugar and sodium. A healthy diet with plenty of fruits and vegetables, accompanied by water, will keep you hydrated.

5. Flavor your water. If you don’t like the taste of plain water, flavor it with fruit such as berries, oranges, lemons, or limes. Cucumber and fresh mint are also popular additions to make a healthy, refreshing and calorie-free beverage. Selzer is a good substitute if you prefer a carbonated beverage, but avoid drinks with additives and sugar.

6. Drink water at mealtime. A glass of water 30 minutes before and one hour after a meal aids digestion. If you eat three times a day and follow this guideline, you’ll have polished off six of your eight glasses of water just with your meals. And drinking water can diminish the appetite for those trying to lose weight.

7. Start every day with a glass of water. Before you reach for that cup of coffee, drink an 8-ounce glass of water. Your body is using water all night while you’re sleeping, so it makes sense that you need to hydrate first thing to get your day started.

Infants and young children are especially vulnerable to dehydration. The primary sign in an infant is a dry diaper, and they may be listless with sunken eyes. Children from 2-5 years of age may be whiny and complain of belly pain, have crusty lips, and also stop urinating.

Common symptoms in adults include fatigue, dizziness, irregular/fast heartbeat, dry skin/chapped lips, and constipation. Check the color of your urine, which should be very pale yellow, like lemonade, when you are properly hydrated. Another self-check for dehydration is to pinch up the skin on the back of your hand, like a tent. If it does not fall flat again right away, you need to drink water.

If you feel disoriented, slur your speech and feel light-headed, your condition is likely extreme and you’ll need IV fluids administered at a health care facility. Babies who do not urinate regularly, become less interactive, have sunken eyes and cracked lips may also require IV fluids. Seek medical attention without delay.

The elderly are particularly at risk for dehydration because they may be unaware they are thirsty and forget to drink enough water. The symptoms of dehydration can mimic dementia or resemble a stroke in seniors. At this point, the person should be taken to the hospital immediately.

People with chronic health conditions need to be especially conscientious about hydration. For those with diabetes, proper nutrition and water intake are key to blood sugar control. Individuals with heart disease, cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, organ transplant recipients, and others who are immune compromised may take medications with diuretic properties that could have affect hydration needs. They should ask their physician for their hydration guidance.

The Health Educator experts at our Montefiore Medical Group adult and pediatric primary care practices can answer your questions about staying hydrated and provide a recommended hydration plan specifically for you and others in your family. Our website also offers free information on a variety of wellness initiatives and includes an array of health information to help you avoid dehydration and stay healthy and safe this summer.
