Prevention of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the best way to

In short

A common cause of winter sniffles, sneezes, runny noses and coughs is respiratory syncytial (sin-SISH-uhl) virus (RSV), which in its milder form resembles the common cold, passing in a week or two. However, RSV presents a significant health challenge to infants and very young children when it causes bronchiolitis, an infection of the smaller airways in the lungs. Bronchiolitis causes these airways, the bronchiole, to swell and become congested with mucus the body produces to fight off the virus. Because baby’s airways are so small, the impact can be much more serious than it is in an older children or adult. Other viruses can cause bronchiolitis, but RSV is the cause in 80% of cases in infants and accounts for most hospitalizations. Understanding how to recognize RSV and bronchiolitis symptoms, deal with the illness, and reduce the risks of viral respiratory infection is important for parents.

RSV is a highly contagious upper respiratory infection most prevalent from October through March. Virtually all children get an RSV infection by the time they are 2 years old. A stuffy nose is usually the first sign, with congestion that progresses down into the lungs, triggering a cough. If the congestion can’t be brought under control, then faster and more difficult breathing follows. Symptoms are usually the worst between the third and fifth day of the illness. It’s natural for parents to feel anxious when their child is having difficulty breathing, and knowing what to expect and when to call the doctor is helpful:

Certain children are more vulnerable to severe RSV infections and complications, including premature infants, young children with congenital heart or lung disease, children with compromised immune systems, and infants in crowded child care settings.

RSV enters the body through the eyes, nose or mouth. The virus is easily spread by respiratory droplets expelled when an infected person coughs or sneezes. RSV can live for hours on hard surfaces such as crib rails, counter tops and toys. Touch your nose, eyes or mouth after touching a contaminated surface, and you can pick up the virus. Unfortunately, there is no vaccine or medication for RSV or any of the viruses that cause bronchiolitis, with the sole exception of influenza, for which there is a vaccine and medicine that may shorten the course of the illness. RSV is usually worst from the third to fifth day, though some symptoms like a cough or runny nose can persist for two or more weeks. The goal is for the child to eat and drink normally and for the severe respiratory distress to lessen.

Again, if your baby is struggling to breathe, has a bluish tint on lips or nail beds, and/or has a persistent fever, seek medical care without delay. The baby may need a nasal cannula to deliver oxygen, hydration therapy, or in rare cases a breathing tube to support their recovery.

Asthma: Parents sometimes ask about asthma treatments, such as an albuterol inhaler, to help their child breathe. Unfortunately, research shows that these medicines are ineffective to treat RSV and bronchiolitis in the majority of the population. If a child has repeated episodes of bronchiolitis, there is, however, an increased risk of developing asthma later in life, particularly when there is a family history of asthma, eczema and allergies. We counsel families with babies who have bronchiolitis to watch for signs of wheezing with future colds, a sign of early-stage asthma.

Pneumonia: RSV is the most common cause of pneumonia as well as bronchiolitis in infants.

Middle ear infection: This occurs when germs enter the space behind the eardrum, which happens most frequently in babies and young children.

Repeat infections: It is not uncommon for RSV to come back around again, even during the same RSV season. However, the symptoms are usually less severe after the initial infection.

Testing for RSV: Children with other chronic illnesses may be more predisposed to RSV, and experience more severe illness from the virus. A rapid viral test exists for RSV and influenza, using a swab of nasal secretions. Results are available within a couple of hours. If the rapid test is negative, the doctor may order a more comprehensive respiratory viral lab panel to look for other viruses that could cause bronchiolitis.

Protective medication: A monoclonal antibody, palivizumab, is available for patients who are at very high risk of complications from RSV. The American Academy of Pediatrics limits the use of this monthly injectable prophylactic to the flu season, for babies born prior to 29 weeks gestation, babies with significant heart, neuromuscular or lung disease, immune-compromised patients, and infants with chronic lung disease.

Montefiore provides expert care to diagnose and treat RSV

While RSV and bronchiolitis may be difficult to prevent for some babies this winter, in the great majority of cases, where a child is otherwise healthy, the symptoms will eventually resolve. If your child is not improving, or is having moderate to severe difficulty breathing, Montefiore’s experts are available to diagnose and treat RSV and its complications at our family practice clinics throughout Westchester and the region. The Children’s Hospital at Montefiore (CHAM) is consistently named among U.S. News & World Report’s “Best Children’s Hospitals” nationally and regionally, providing state-of-the-art health care for children from our area, across the nation and around the world. You’ll find more detailed information on RSV and bronchiolitis symptoms and care at the CHAM site.

Alyssa H. Silver, M.D., is an attending physician at the Children’s Hospital at Montefiore and an assistant professor of pediatrics at Albert Einstein College of Medicine. As a hospitalist, Dr. Silver optimizes the care of hospitalized children. In doing so, she provides the best, evidence-based care, while partnering with patients and their families to maximize outcomes and minimize the discomforts of being in the hospital.
