Chayote Squash takes center stage in this Tinola Chicken Stew

Reviewed by Jessica Shapiro, MS, Montefiore · 2 min read

In short

Each year, during the month of March we celebrate all things nutrition because it is National Nutrition Month. In the northeast, the temperatures are still low, and there is nothing more nutritious than a nice bowl of hot soup or stew. This traditional Filipino recipe is a tasty and healthy option loaded with protein, minerals, vitamins, and fiber. Embracing cultures and flavors from all over the world provides tremendous versatility and endless ingredient options to nourish our bodies.

  • Reviewed by Jessica Shapiro, MS, Montefiore Einstein

Chayote takes center stage in the Tinola Chicken Stew. Chayote originated in Mexico and is very common in Latin American cuisines. It can be seen incorporated in dishes from around the world, albeit it may be referred to by one of many other names, such as vegetable pear or mirliton.

Here are some interesting facts about the chayote to be aware of as you enjoy this crave-worthy stew on a chilly day:

1. Chayote is a fruit that is relatively easy to find and prepare.

If you haven’t looked for Chayote before you might not even have noticed it in the produce section of your larger supermarket or specialty grocery store! Look out for the pear-shaped squash with lumpy bright green skin and lumpy ridges on it. Some chayote variations are prickly. In some parts of the world, like Mexico, the fruit, stem, and young leaves are eaten raw, boiled, or used as a complementary ingredient to a favorite recipe.

Although botanically a fruit (like cucumbers or melons), Chayote is treated culinarily much like a summer squash, such as zucchini and pattypan. It is more popular in cooked dishes, even though “the word "squash" derives from the term askutasquash, which translates to "a green thing eaten raw,” according to the New World Encyclopedia.

Whether consumed cooked or raw the chayote’s mild flavor lends itself to be very versatile, delicious in both sweet and savory dishes. Raw chayote is crisp and crunchy, like cucumber or jicama, so it is a wonderful addition to a salad. 2. The nutritional value of chayote is unquestionable.

The chayote is mostly made of water so it is lower in calories and has minimal fat. However, it is full of potent antioxidants and vitamins, and minerals. Notable standouts include fiber, vitamin C, potassium, and folate. The fruit and seeds are also rich with many amino acids. Keep in mind that if you cook the chayote, the water-soluble vitamins such as vitamin C will diminish a bit. Chayote has also been used for medicinal purposes. Consumption of the leaves is believed to reduce kidney stones and may lower blood pressure.

Celebrate the nutritional value and adaptability of chayote this month with a recipe from a cuisine that includes the flavors you enjoy. You also won’t go wrong with this mouth-watering and flavorful Tinola Chicken Stew recipe.
