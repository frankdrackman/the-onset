HIFU, other latest prostate cancer treatments can preserve sexual function

By Kara L. Watts, MD, Director of Urology Quality Assurance & Improvement and Director of Prostate Cancer Screening Program · 3 min read · El HIFU y otros tratamientos de cáncer de próstata logran preservar la función sexual

In short

One of the most common concerns about treating prostate cancer is preserving sexual function and urinary control. For some prostate cancers, newer treatments offering an alternative to radiation and radical surgery can preserve erections and bladder control. In some cases, early prostate cancer can be safely monitored without intervention. Although many find it difficult to talk about, discussing prostate cancer screening with your doctor is essential to catching it early, particularly as there are often no symptoms in its earliest stages. Knowing about the latest treatment options, risk factors, and how to detect prostate cancer early can help prepare you for a conversation with your doctor.

Catching prostate cancer early may open the door to avoiding radical surgery and radiation

New treatments, known as focal ablation therapy, are an alternative to prostate removal surgery and radiation. These minimally invasive treatment options use state-of-the-art imaging technologies to identify and precisely target cancerous tissue, reducing damage to surrounding healthy tissue. Screening for prostate cancer most often starts with a PSA blood test. This blood test measures the level of a substance called prostate-specific antigen (PSA), which is produced in the prostate. The PSA level can be elevated when prostate cancer is present, although other reasons may also cause the level to rise, including increasing age, benign prostate enlargement, inflammation or infection of the prostate, taking certain medicines, use of herbal supplements, and some medical procedures. Some medications and herbal supplements can suppress PSA levels, even when cancer is present.

Prostate cancer differs from other cancers in how it is screened for, so understanding the disease and your risks is important.

An abnormal PSA blood test cannot diagnose prostate cancer but should lead to a referral to a urologist for further evaluation. A urologist may then recommend additional tests, such as additional urine or blood tests and/or an MRI scan of the prostate.

Prostate cancer differs from other cancers in how it is screened for, so understanding the disease and your risks is important. There are several types of prostate cancer and a growing understanding of genetic risk factors. Those at higher risk for aggressive prostate cancer — including African Americans, those with a family history of prostate and other cancers, and people with the BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene mutations — should discuss prostate cancer screening with their doctor when they reach their forties.
