Understanding bedwetting — expert advice for effective strategies

By Miriam Harel, MD, Attending Physician, Pediatric Urology · 3 min read · Incontinencia nocturna: consejos de expertos para estrategias eficaces

In short

Waking up to wet sheets is a nightly reality for many families. Roughly 1 in 5 five-year-olds and 1 in 10 seven-year-olds still experience bedwetting, making it one of childhood’s most common, if least talked-about, developmental stages. Most children eventually outgrow it, but along the way, there are effective strategies that can help transition to dry nights, starting with an understanding of common reasons why most kids wet the bed.

Steps toward dry nights and when to seek help

1. Help them understand what’s behind bedwetting. When kids understand what’s behind bedwetting, it’s easier to work together on solutions. Most kids feel relieved and hopeful when they’re reassured that bedwetting is not their fault and is nothing to be embarrassed about. Here’s what’s behind bedwetting for most kids:

2. Have kids use the bathroom twice before bedtime — once 30 minutes prior and again just before lights-out — to ensure they’re going to sleep with an empty bladder.

3. Relax — and blow out — while urinating. Children don’t always empty their bladder completely when they urinate. To help them learn, parents can encourage them to relax, take their time, and whistle or pretend to blow out candles on a birthday cake. Both actions put gentle action on the bladder and often do the trick.

4. Practice bladder training. Scheduling bathroom breaks every 2 to 3 hours throughout the day, whether or not kids think they have to go, can help improve bladder control.

5. Limit liquids 2 hours before bedtime. While it’s important that children stay hydrated throughout the day, limiting what they drink 2 hours before bedtime can help prevent excessive urine production while they’re sleeping.

6. Avoid or minimize beverages that irritate the bladder. Soda, fruit juice, and caffeinated drinks irritate the bladder, increasing urgency and frequency of urination, so it’s best to avoid them, especially in the evening.

7. Make dietary changes that support regular bowel movements. Infrequent, irregular, and hard bowel movements can be signs that a child is constipated, and addressing the issue can be enough to eliminate bedwetting. Children can avoid constipation by drinking more during the day (around 5 cups of water are recommended daily for kids ages 4 to 8) and eating more fiber, including fruits, vegetables, and whole grains. If constipation persists, your child’s pediatrician may recommend stool softeners.

When more help is needed — or any time you’re concerned about a child’s bedwetting — you can talk to your pediatrician, who may recommend additional solutions like nighttime medications that temporarily limit urine production. These can be prescribed as a safe and effective short-term solution for children 6 and older. Some doctors recommend bedwetting alarms, which include a small moisture sensor that is placed in the child’s underwear or on their bed. Over time, bedwetting alarms can help stop accidents in real time, waking kids at the first sign of wetness so they can get up and use the bathroom.

Doctors can also rule out other health issues that contribute to bedwetting. In some cases, bedwetting is related to trauma, stress, or behavioral issues, so psychological counseling may be recommended. It’s especially important to speak with a doctor if bedwetting is something new — starting suddenly after at least six months of dry nights — and happens alongside daytime accidents or painful urination, which can be signs of a urinary tract infection or other medical condition.
