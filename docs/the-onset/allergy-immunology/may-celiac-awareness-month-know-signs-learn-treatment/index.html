May is Celiac Awareness Month. Know the signs, learn about the

By Dr. Jennifer Katz, Allergy & Immunology · 4 min read

In short

Found in grains such as wheat, barley and rye, gluten is a protein that gets mixed reviews when it comes to your health. For three million or more U.S. residents with celiac disease, eating foods containing gluten can trigger an autoimmune response in the body, causing inflammation and damage to the small intestine. Left untreated, celiac disease can have a lasting impact on your overall health, yet 97% of cases go undiagnosed.

Celiac Awareness Month in May sets the table for us to learn more about the symptoms, risks and treatment of celiac disease.

On the upside, once diagnosed, celiac disease is completely treatable. Complete avoidance of foods containing gluten is usually all that’s needed to eliminate symptoms, heal the damage, and prevent future episodes. Read on to learn about the importance of an accurate diagnosis and what to expect.

While celiac disease symptoms can vary greatly and show up differently in children and adults, the most common issues for adults include diarrhea, fatigue and weight loss, as well as bloating, gas, abdominal pain, nausea, constipation and vomiting. If you experience diarrhea or digestive issues that persist beyond two weeks, it is important to consult a doctor. Wherever possible, seek out a specialist, usually a gastroenterologist, who has expertise in testing, diagnosing and treating celiac disease. Be sure to mention any of the subtle ways you may be experiencing discomfort, to help your doctor determine the next steps.

There are two steps to diagnosis of celiac disease. First is the screening portion, which is a simple blood test. People who have celiac disease and eat foods containing gluten are found to have higher than normal levels of certain antibodies in their blood. The antibodies show up because your body reacts to gluten as a threat — and the key element of this test is making sure your diet contains gluten. Remember: Gluten is a protein found in wheat, barley and rye, so you should be eating these grains, or foods that contain them or gluten additives, prior to the blood test to get an accurate result.

If the screening indicates an immune response to gluten is present, the next step in the process solidifies the celiac disease diagnosis through an intestinal biopsy, or endoscopy. The endoscopy is a procedure that allows your doctor to see what is going on inside the gastrointestinal tract through a scope placed in the small intestine. This step is important, as it allows doctors to see if the symptoms improve on a gluten-free diet due to a placebo effect, or if there is a different gastrointestinal issue.

Once you are screened and diagnosed with celiac disease, the treatment is straightforward for most people: Follow a gluten-free diet. The fact that so many foods contain gluten can make this a challenge — and even a tiny amount of gluten hidden in a food product can trigger an immune reaction. You’ll find a lot of support from nutritionists, and the growing number of gluten-free options at the grocery store and restaurants are making the experience both easier and more palatable. Fresh fruit, vegetables, and meats are naturally gluten free, and many new gluten-free products are available to replace traditional prepared foods and baked goods. In most cases, following this diet can stop symptoms and heal existing intestinal damage, making the effort worthwhile.

Treating celiac disease by avoidance of gluten-containing foods is a lifetime commitment. Left untreated, celiac disease has a lasting negative impact on a person’s health; it can lead to malnutrition, loss of bone density, lactose intolerance, and even cancer and neurological problems. It is important to stay the gluten-free course after diagnosis to ensure your body gets all that it needs.

Celiac disease symptoms can be painful and frustrating, and the cause of the disorder does not make it any easier to understand. Doctors know it is caused by an interaction between genes and eating foods containing gluten, but the precise reason behind celiac disease remains unknown. Gastrointestinal infections and gut bacteria may play a role in developing the disease, and it can also be triggered after surgery, pregnancy, childbirth and viral infections.

There is a genetic component that impacts a person’s risk of developing the disease, but having certain gene variants does not mean you are guaranteed to develop the disease later on in life. The most common risk factors for developing celiac disease include having a family member with the disease, Type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, Addison’s disease, Down syndrome or Turner syndrome. Although these risk factors may make developing celiac disease more common, it can impact anyone.

If you believe you may have celiac disease, speak with a doctor about starting the process of testing. Diagnosis and treatment could involve a number of services, all of which can be found at Montefiore. In addition to highly respected adult and pediatric gastroenterology programs, Montefiore has a state-of-the-art immunodiagnostic lab on premises for rapid and accurate test results, along with specially trained clinicians to support your care. Celiac disease is a serious condition that causes your immune system to respond negatively to certain foods you eat, but the good news is it is completely treatable, and following a gluten-free diet can help you conquer the symptoms and recover your health.
