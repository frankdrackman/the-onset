Stay ahead of colon cancer by screening at age 45

By Edward Chu, M.D., Cancer Care · 6 min read

In short

When caught early, colorectal cancer (CRC) is easily treated and has a survival rate of over 90%. At the same time, it is the cancer that people really don’t want to talk about — even with their doctor. In 2022, approximately 150,000 new cases will be diagnosed and nearly 53,000 deaths will occur, many of them avoidable. Early detection is the key, and screening is how we save lives. Here’s what you need to know to talk with your doctor about options for colorectal cancer screening and risk factors.

Experts now recommend getting a first colonoscopy five years sooner. Here’s why.

For the past 15 years, the general recommendation has been for CRC screening to start at age 50 for average-risk individuals. However, with an increasing incidence of CRC in the younger population, the screening guidelines put forth by the American Cancer Society have changed, and the new recommendation is for individuals at average risk of CRC to start regular screening at age 45, and for those who are high risk to begin before age 45. For individuals in good health, regular CRC screening should continue through age 75. For those ages 76 to 85, screening should be based on a conversation with your doctor, including consideration of the presence of other existing illnesses and previous screening history. CRC screening is not recommended for individuals over age 85.

While we have always thought of family history as the main risk factor for developing colorectal cancer, age is actually the number one risk factor, with more than 90% of cases diagnosed in adults over age 50. Thanks to widespread screening of this group, the past 10 to 15 years have seen a gradual decrease in mortality. Still, more than one-third of adults over 50 are not up to date with recommended screenings, which is one reason why CRC continues to be the second most common cause of cancer death. In addition, there has been a significant increase in CRC among people younger than 50, with a parallel increase in death rates. This is why colorectal cancer continues to be a significant public health challenge as well as a personal tragedy for far too many people.

In addition to age, other important risk factors are a personal history of CRC or certain types of polyps, family history of CRC or hereditary forms of CRC, and history of inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s colitis or ulcerative colitis). An individual’s risk for developing CRC is two to three times greater with a first-degree relative, and the risk goes up to six times greater when two first-degree relatives have CRC. As a result, it is important that your doctor be up to date on your family history to help you make the best decision about screening.

Racial and ethnic background also plays an important role in CRC risk. African Americans have the highest CRC incidence and mortality rates of all racial groups in the United States. It is important to know that early-onset CRC is diagnosed up to two times more frequently in Blacks and Hispanics than in Caucasians. In addition, Blacks and Hispanics have significantly reduced survival rates as compared with Caucasians. Another at-risk group is Ashkenazi Jews of Eastern European descent, who have one of the highest risks of developing CRC of any ethnic group in the world.

Lifestyle factors also impact your risk for colorectal cancer. It is clear that diets rich in processed meats and high in fat, while low in fruits, vegetables and dietary fiber, contribute to the increased incidence of CRC. Other factors that may increase risk include smoking, alcohol use, obesity and physical inactivity. Diabetes is also felt to be a contributing factor to CRC risk, especially for individuals with Type 2 diabetes.

It has been commonly thought that CRC is a man’s disease. In fact, this is not true —the incidence of CRC is nearly identical in men and women.

Colon cancer grows from polyps in the colon, so removing them stops that from occurring. They are usually benign in the beginning and typically take eight to 10 years to develop into a real cancer.

There are several options for testing for colorectal cancer that fall into two groups: visual/structural tests of the colon and rectum, and stool-based tests.

Let’s start with the gold standard: colonoscopy. During this outpatient procedure, the gastroenterologist is able to directly visualize the entire colon and rectum region, identify and remove polyps, and biopsy any mass that is present. The value of colonoscopy cannot be overstated, as this is the test that can both remove polyps and identify CRC at its earliest stages. When caught at an early stage, CRC can be cured in more than 90% of cases.

A virtual colonoscopy is an effective diagnostic tool. It uses an advanced CT (computerized tomography) scan to generate a 3D image of the colon and surrounding organs. Radiologists can then look for any potential problems. These scans eliminate many of the cumbersome aspects of a traditional colonoscopy. Home prep is easy, and the patient can drive themselves home after the scan. It is an option for people who can’t be sedated because of preexisting conditions. If a polyp or lesion is found, the patient will then need to have a traditional colonoscopy.

There are other visual/structural tests your doctor may recommend, such as flexible sigmoidoscopy.

Stool-based tests screen for colon cancer. These include a FIT (fecal immunohistochemical test) and gFOBT (guaiac-based fecal occult blood test), both of which look for hidden blood in the stool, a possible early sign of cancer. Another test is the multi-targeted stool test, which identifies DNA changes in the cells of a stool sample. The advantage of each of these screening tests is that they can be done in the comfort of one’s home, which is very convenient for patients. It is important to know that in the event of a positive result, a colonoscopy will need to be done to investigate the possibility of CRC. There is also the possibility of a false positive reading. Still, a stool-based test is an acceptable option for people who are at average risk for colorectal cancer and have no symptoms.

The bottom line on testing: Your doctor will recommend the best screening for you based on your individual situation.

The typical symptoms for CRC include fatigue, reduced energy, loss of appetite, weight loss, abdominal pain or discomfort, blood in stools or change in the color of stools, as well as a change in the size, shape and/or consistency of stools. If you experience these symptoms, set up an appointment to discuss them with your doctor as soon as you can. Some of this conversation may feel embarrassing, but it is a discussion that could save your life.

Some patients experience no symptoms at all, and this is why CRC screening should be done as a matter of health maintenance for all adults. For people at average risk, a colon cancer screening at age 45 is recommended. For individuals who have no polyps or other issues, the screening interval will most likely be 10 years. If polyps are found, or any other abnormalities, that interval will be shorter. If racial or ethnic background places you at higher risk, you’ve had symptoms or you have a family history, don’t wait until you’re 45. Talk with your doctor about screening for colorectal cancer. Screening saves lives.

Montefiore Einstein integrates groundbreaking research with cutting-edge cancer care

Montefiore Einstein Cancer Center’s patient-centered and evidence-based cancer care addresses the unique needs of each individual patient. One of the top 5% of all accredited clinical cancer centers in the United States to be designated by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and ranked in the top 1% of all hospitals in the nation for cancer by U.S. News & World Report, our NCI-designated cancer center brings the worlds of clinical care and academic research together and allows for the translation of novel cutting-edge scientific discoveries into improved clinical outcomes and new lifesaving treatments.

Current research includes identifying novel targets or pathways critical for the growth of colon cancer. By identifying these potential targets, we are able to develop new drugs or combination treatments to slow the growth of or kill the colon cancer through these pathways. Our investigators are also studying the genomics of colorectal cancer in our Bronx patient population, as there is growing evidence that the molecular alterations in our minority underserved patients are different from those of Caucasian white patients. Montefiore Einstein researchers are also studying the underlying mechanisms that contribute to resistance to current treatments.
