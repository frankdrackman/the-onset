Beyond the Pap: Self-collection now a cervical cancer testing option

By Mark Einstein, MD, FACS, FACOG, Department Chair of Obstetrics & Gynecology and Women’s Health · 5 min read

In short

Cervical cancer is often curable when diagnosed in its initial stages and is highly preventable if pre-cancerous abnormalities are caught early. Yet many individuals diagnosed with cervical cancer in the U.S. have seen a medical provider in the previous five years but were not screened for cervical cancer. Human papillomavirus (HPV), the virus that causes nearly all cervical cancers, and any pre-cancerous changes can be detected with HPV tests and Pap smears. Continued advancements to increase accessibility for cervical cancer testing now include the newly approved self-collection HPV tests that don’t need to be done with a traditional pelvic exam. These tests are becoming available in a broad range of medical settings, not just the gynecology office. Imagine primary care, urgent care, or emergency room visits now providing an opportunity for cervical cancer screening. Here’s what you need to know about the latest testing options, prevention, and risk factors.

Understanding the new HPV test that can be done without a standard pelvic exam

Why is the new self-collection option so important?

Thousands of preventable cervical cancer deaths occur each year because more than 30% of eligible women in the U.S. don’t undergo regular screenings. Some women avoid Pap smears and HPV tests because of the need for an uncomfortable pelvic exam or have difficulty fitting in such an exam in their busy schedule due to children, work, and many other things. Prevention winds up being lower on the priority list. To address this, experts hope to increase screening rates by ensuring access to self-collection HPV tests in nontraditional office settings or situations where a pelvic exam causes anxiety for a variety of reasons, including past sexual trauma, not identifying as a woman, religious and cultural beliefs, or discomfort.

Women aged 30 and over have the option for self-collection of samples to test for HPV in a wide range of health-care settings. If you received a positive HPV test result from self-collection, you would still need to receive follow-up care often with a generalist ob-gyn. This innovative approach gives women the option to collect their own samples in office settings, reducing barriers to testing and with the promise of increasing screening rates.

HPV self-collection in a health care setting could be the first step to making at-home testing available, which would further increase access to screening. Montefiore Einstein was selected as one of the lead U.S. sites testing home-collected HPV testing as part of a National Cancer Institute clinical trial.

Know the risk factors and symptoms of cervical cancer

Getting an HPV infection is the most common risk factor, but it’s very common, with approximately 75% of Americans getting exposed during their lifetime. The vast majority of women who have been infected with HPV will never get cervical cancer or precancer, but having it persistently over time puts you in a higher-risk category. If you have other risk factors on top of HPV infection — such as being immunocompromised, taking immunosuppressant medication, and smoking — your chance of developing cervical cancer increases even more.

Women with cervical cancer often have no symptoms, which is why it’s vital to stay on top of screenings. If symptoms do occur, it may be because the cancer has advanced.

The sooner cervical cancer is diagnosed and treated, the more effective treatment is.

Years ago, HPV was contracted by most sexually active men and women, but according to the CDC, primary prevention of cervical cancer using the HPV vaccine has been turning those numbers around. The American Cancer Society reports that, from 2012 to 2019, cervical cancer rates in women between the ages 20 and 24dropped by a remarkable 65%. The vaccine has been shown to be safe and is most effective in preventing the virus in people who have not yet been exposed, meaning those who are not yet sexually active. While the vaccine is now available for people up to age 45, cervical cancer could mostly be eliminated if the majority of people received it at an early age. Though men can’t get cervical cancer, vaccination helps prevent the spread of the virus and prevents some noncervical cancers, like anal and head and neck, which can also result from HPV.
