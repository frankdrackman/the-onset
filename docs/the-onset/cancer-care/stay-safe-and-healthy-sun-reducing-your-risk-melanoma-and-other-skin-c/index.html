Stay safe and healthy in the sun by reducing your risk of melanoma

By Dr. Pamela Schutzer Jakubowicz, Cancer Care · 4 min read

In short

National Skin Cancer and Melanoma Awareness Month in May reminds us how important it is to reduce our risk of skin cancer while enjoying the summer sunshine. One in five US residents will develop skin cancer by age 70, making it the most common of all cancers. In fact, more skin cancers are diagnosed here each year than all other cancers combined. One type, melanoma, is of particular concern and it is on the increase. When diagnosed and treated early, melanoma has a 98% cure rate, yet thousands of Americans will lose their lives to melanoma this year. Protecting yourself from skin cancer involves shielding your skin from exposure to the sun’s burning rays, and regularly checking your skin to search for any signs of cancer. Fortunately, both these steps are easy to do.

Up to 90% of skin cancers are directly attributable to exposure to sunlight or artificial tanning lights. The ultraviolet A (UVA) and ultraviolet B (UVB) components of the sun’s radiation are the culprits. UVA causes wrinkles and premature aging of the skin while UVB causes burns, and both of them can lead to cancer. The 5 S’s were originally created in Australia in response to a dramatic rise in cases of melanoma. After almost thirty years, declining skin cancer rates have proven the effectiveness of this combination of measures, all of which come down to blocking harmful UVA and UVB rays.

Physically blocking the sun with clothing, hats, and sunglasses is an easy way to protect yourself. Specially designed clothing is increasingly available with a much higher ultraviolet protection factor (UPF) than regular street clothes, and this can be especially helpful for days spent outdoors at the beach, pool, or on the water. There is even a laundry additive that you can add that will increase the UPF of your regular clothes.

Reducing your exposure to the damaging rays of the sun is helpful at any age, but most of us have been exposed since childhood — and that means we need to regularly check our skin for any signs of trouble.

Broad spectrum sunscreen is available today in a variety of formulas with different ingredients, and it should be worn every day. Look for a sun protection factor (SPF) of 15 for daily use, and SPF 30 or higher for extended outdoor activities. Apply one ounce, or two tablespoons, a half hour before heading outside. Reapply after you’ve been outside for half an hour, then every two hours for as long as you’re in the sun. Also reapply after swimming or sweating a lot.

Finally, seek shade whenever possible, especially between the hours of 10 a.m. and 3 p.m. when the sun is strongest. Remember, that even a single severe sunburn can increase your chance of getting skin cancer later in life, and a tan is a sign of skin damage, not good health.

Reducing your exposure to the damaging rays of the sun is helpful at any age, but most of us have been exposed since childhood — and that means we need to regularly check our skin for any signs of trouble. It’s easy to mistake a melanoma for a benign mole, which is why an annual check-up with a dermatologist is recommended. Regular self-checks increase the chance of early detection, and this is where the ABCDEs are helpful in knowing when to visit your doctor.

Early detection and prompt treatment of melanoma saves lives, while in less aggressive skin cancers it reduces the chance of disfigurement or other complications.

Skin cancer can happen to anyone, even those who tan easily or have darker skin tones. That’s because the sun damages the DNA in the pigment cells of your skin. The pigment cells absorb UV radiation and repair themselves to protect your skin, but after too much exposure, they may become unable to repair themselves and the DNA changes, leading to the growth of cancerous cells. The risk of sun damage is higher for Caucasians, and those with fair skin, blonde or red hair, blue or green eyes, or skin that burns or freckles easily are at the greatest risk. Moles are harmless and may never develop cancer, but many moles can increase the risk for melanoma. African Americans are susceptible to a certain type of melanoma that is very aggressive and shows up on the soles of the feet, palms of the hands, or nail beds. Anyone who works outside or spends a lot of time in the sun is at higher risk, and age is also a factor due to the cumulative nature of exposure to the sun’s radiation. If you are in a high risk group, an annual skin check by a dermatologist is a must, and any spot you find that fits the ABCDE criteria should be looked at by your doctor as soon as possible.

When you visit your dermatologist for a full skin check, the doctor will thoroughly examine your skin with a bright light and magnifier. The location and size of any moles or spots will be noted and rechecked for changes at a future visit. If cancer is suspected anywhere, the doctor will take a sample of cells to examine. If this biopsy comes back positive, your treatment will depend on the type of cancer detected and whether or not it has spread to other areas.

At Montefiore, skin cancer and melanoma patients benefit from customized treatment plans prepared by a team of experts including medical, surgical and radiation oncologists and orthopedic surgeons who consult with clinical experts in dermatology, plastic surgery, pathology, nutrition and nuclear medicine. Montefiore offers innovative treatment options including immunotherapies, as well as access to early-stage studies of biologics not available anywhere else. The Center for Cancer Care provides a full spectrum of support for patients in a family-centered environment, including alternative support services.

Remember to check and protect your skin — and enjoy the beautiful summer days ahead!
