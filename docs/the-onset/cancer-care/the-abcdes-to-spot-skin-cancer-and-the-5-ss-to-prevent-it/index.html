The ABCDEs to spot skin cancer and the 5 S's to prevent it

By Beth N. McLellan, MD, Chief of Dermatology · 5 min read · El ABCDE para detectar el cáncer de piel y cinco medidas para prevenirlo

In short

Warmer weather and more outside fun mean it’s the perfect time to review the 5 S’s of Sun Safety — simple ways to protect your skin and reduce the risk of skin cancer in people of all ages and skin tones. Skin cancer can also be hard to spot, showing up in unexpected places like your armpits and even between your toes. So follow the ABCDE’s of spotting skin cancer to catch it early, when it’s most treatable. Here’s how to spot it and keep your skin safe this summer and all year round.

Skin cancer can be hard to spot. Check these less obvious places.

The 5 S’s — simple steps that reduce skin cancer risk

The steps you can take to lower your risk of skin cancer may not be new to you, but they have proven effective at reducing risk.

Up to 90% of skin cancers are directly attributable to exposure to sunlight or artificial tanning lights. The ultraviolet A (UVA) and ultraviolet B (UVB) components of the sun’s radiation are the culprits. UVA causes wrinkles and premature aging of the skin while UVB causes burns, and both of them can lead to cancer. A combination of strategies to avoid exposure to UVA and UVB is outlined in the 5 S’s.

The 5 S’s were originally created in Australia in response to a dramatic rise in cases of melanoma. After almost 30 years, declining skin cancer rates have proven the effectiveness of this combination of measures, all of which come down to blocking harmful UVA and UVB rays.

Use sunscreen with “broad spectrum” on the label, for protection against UVA and UVB radiation. Seek products with at least SPF 30. Reapply every two hours. For people with more sensitive skin, consider using physical or mineral blocker SPF ingredients, such as zinc oxide and/or titanium oxide.

Physically blocking the sun with clothing, hats, and sunglasses is an easy way to protect yourself. Specially designed clothing is increasingly available with a much higher ultraviolet protection factor (UPF) than regular street clothes, and this can be especially helpful for days spent outdoors at the beach, at the pool, or on the water. There is even a laundry additive that you can add that will increase the UPF of your regular clothes.

Sun protection is important year-round, even during winter. Make it easy by choosing a moisturizer with sunscreen, so you do everything in one step.

Remember, sunscreen is for everyone, not just for people with fair skin. Not only does it decrease your risk of skin cancer, it also protects against dark spots, wrinkles, and other signs of aging.

The more often you examine your skin, the more familiar you’ll become with what’s normal on your body. Have your dermatologist check out anything that looks bigger, darker, or different than your other spots — or if it’s evolved in the past month.

Perform these monthly at-home skin checks in front of a full-length mirror. Look at your front and back with your arms raised so you won’t miss anything. Check under your arms, on the soles of your feet and the palms of your hands, and between your toes. Use a hand mirror to examine the back of your neck and as much of your scalp as possible. You might want to enlist a partner or friend to take a look at your back and scalp. For those who wear nail polish, make sure to take a quick look for any new marks under your finger and toe nails before you apply a new coat of polish.

Self-checks are especially important for those at higher risk of skin cancer, such as people who are immune compromised, people who have had skin cancer before and people with a strong family history of skin cancer.

It is still recommended to see a dermatologist for a full-body skin check once a year, because dermatologists have a well-trained eye and can check hard-to-view places like the back of your neck and seemingly hidden patches.

If you don’t have a dermatologist, please talk to your primary care physician about annual skin checks and a referral to a dermatologist.

Chronic sun exposure and a history of blistering sunburns increases your risk of skin cancer regardless of gender or ethnicity. Skin cancer can develop in places that get lots of sun exposure, like the face, neck, and arms, as well as in parts that see little or no direct sun, like your buttocks or the soles of your feet.

Two types of skin cancer — basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma — are very common. They may initially look like a non-healing pimple or a dry, rough patch, but if those types of lesions don’t return to normal within three to four weeks, have a dermatologist check them out.

Melanoma is a more serious, yet less common, form of skin cancer that often appears as new or changing brown spots. Those spots can even show up under fingernails and toenails. And although people who have fair skin and are more susceptible to sunburn can be at higher risk, individuals with darker skin are still at risk.
