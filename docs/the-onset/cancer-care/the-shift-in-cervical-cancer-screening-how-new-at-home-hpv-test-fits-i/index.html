The shift in cervical cancer screening: How new at-home HPV test fits in

By Mark Einstein, MD, FACS, FACOG, Department Chair of Obstetrics & Gynecology and Women’s Health · 6 min read · Nueva prueba del VPH desde casa para detectar el cáncer de cuello uterino

In short

Today, HPV testing remains one of the preferred ways to screen for cervical cancer — and now the new at‑home HPV test brings that screening right into your home, reflecting continued advancements to increase accessibility for cervical cancer testing. Under the latest American Cancer Society guidelines, women can use a self‑collected HPV swab test, some available for using at home or self-collected in a healthcare setting, such as a doctor’s office. Clinician‑collected HPV tests during a pelvic exam are still preferred when possible, typically every five years, but home self‑testing every three years offers a convenient, guideline‑supported alternative.

New in-home HPV test option is now available, joining healthcare-setting Pap and HPV tests

Cervical cancer can often be cured when caught early, and it’s highly preventable when pre-cancerous abnormalities are detected through screening. Yet many people diagnosed with cervical cancer haven’t been screened within the previous five years. Here’s what you need to know about the latest testing options, updated screening guidelines, plus how to prevent cervical cancer and who’s at greatest risk. But let’s start with why it’s so important to screen for HPV.

HPV (human papillomavirus) causes nearly all cervical cancers. It’s the main risk factor, but it’s also very common — about 75% of Americans are exposed at some point in their lives. The good news is that most women who get HPV never develop cervical cancer or even precancerous changes. The concern is when HPV doesn’t clear the body for many years; this persistent infection raises your risk. That risk goes up further if you also smoke, have a weakened immune system, or take medications that suppress your system.

Your cervical cancer screening options (and what’s new)

The latest American Cancer Society cervical cancer screening guidelines, updated in December 2025, now recommend screening starting at age 25 vs. 21 and continuing until at least 65 for people at average risk. People over 65 can speak with their doctor to determine if further testing is needed.

Here are the new recommended screening options.

The at-home HPV test, the first one approved in May 2025, offers a comfortable and convenient option for people who prefer to collect a sample in private — whether that’s because of scheduling challenges, discomfort or anxiety with pelvic exams, past sexual trauma, or cultural or religious reasons. Available either in a healthcare setting or through an online visit, the test allows you to collect a vaginal swab yourself and mail it to a lab for analysis. If the result is negative, the test should be repeated every 3 years. If it’s positive, you’ll need to see a healthcare provider for further testing.

2. Healthcare-setting self-collection HPV test

Self-collected vaginal swab tests can also be done privately in a doctor’s office or other healthcare settings — such as primary care clinics, urgent care centers, and emergency rooms — without a provider inserting it. Like at-home tests, they offer a comfortable option for those who would rather not have a pelvic exam or who cannot make time for one. Also like the at-home test, if the result is negative, this type of HPV test should be repeated every 3 years. If positive, you will need to see a healthcare provider for further testing.

Preferred by the American Cancer Society, primary HPV testing uses cervical cells collected by a healthcare provider during a pelvic exam. If the result is negative, the test should be repeated every 5 years. If positive, you will need to see a doctor for further testing.

The HPV test and Pap test taken together in one procedure — checks for both high-risk HPV and abnormal cervical cells that might be precancerous. This has to be collected in the usual way by a healthcare provider. Co-testing should be repeated every 5 years. Like the primary HPV test, if the result is negative the test should be repeated every 5 years. If you test positive for HPV, or abnormal cervical cells, further testing is needed.

A Pap test alone is now only recommended when other testing approaches aren’t available. If negative, the test should be repeated every 3 years. If abnormal cervical cells are found they could be pre-cancerous, so further testing is needed.

Screening guidelines exceptions: Who needs more — or less — frequent screening?

Several factors — such as medical history paired with the test type and results — are taken into consideration to determine when and how frequently to get screened. The decline in cervical cancer: What’s a key factor?

Years ago, HPV was contracted by most sexually active men and women, but according to the CDC, primary prevention of cervical cancer using the HPV vaccine has been turning those numbers around. The American Cancer Society reports that, from 2012 to 2019, cervical cancer rates in women between the ages 20 and 24 dropped by a remarkable 65%. The vaccine has been shown to be safe and is most effective in preventing the virus in people who have not yet been exposed, meaning those who are not yet sexually active. While the vaccine is now available for people up to age 45, cervical cancer could mostly be eliminated through primarily preventing HPV if the majority of people received it at an early age, prior to exposure to HPV. Though men can’t get cervical cancer, vaccination helps prevent the spread of the virus and prevents some noncervical cancers, like anal and head and neck, which can also result from a persistent HPV infection.

Women with precancers of the cervix have no symptoms, which is why it’s vital to stay on top of screenings. If symptoms do occur, it may be because a cancer is present.

The sooner cervical cancer is diagnosed and treated, the more effective treatment is.
