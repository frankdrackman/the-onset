Thyroid myths vs. facts: Everything you need to know

By Vafa Tabatabaie, MD, Clinical Director for Endocrinology and Associate Professor, Endocrinology and Orthopedics · 5 min read

In short

Up to 60% of people with thyroid disease are unaware of their condition and struggle to recognize the symptoms, which can be challenging in part because misinformation easily found online can lead to misunderstanding what might be wrong. The thyroid is a small gland in the lower front of your neck that produces a hormone that regulates your metabolism. When something goes wrong with the thyroid and its role in producing this hormone is thrown off, it can lead to symptoms like fatigue, muscle aches and weakness, and unexplained weight loss or some weight gain. With so much confusing information out there, debunking the myths is the key to understanding your thyroid.

With so much misinformation out there about the thyroid, it’s important to know the facts

Myth 1: Thyroid issues are responsible for most cases of unexpected weight gain

The facts: It’s true that hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid) can lead to some weight gain.But the thyroid is not the only controller of weight. There are many other factors that contribute to your body’s energy use, food intake, and weight. When you’re unsure if weight gain is because of your thyroid, ask your doctor. Thyroid disease is easily diagnosed with a blood test that measures your hormone levels.

The facts: If your thyroid is healthy, eating a normal diet is highly unlikely to damage it.

Myth 2: If I’m tired all the time, I must have thyroid problems

The facts: Most thyroid disease symptoms, including fatigue, are nonspecific, meaning they could be caused by any number of things. For example, fatigue can also be due to poor sleep, anemia, or certain medications. If you have a thyroid problem, you’re likely to notice multiple symptoms, not just one. Symptoms of an underactive thyroid include fatigue, feeling cold all the time, weight gain, constipation, very dry skin, and hair loss. Symptoms of an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) include nervousness, irritability, fatigue, increased appetite, rapid or irregular heartbeat, and goiter (an enlarged thyroid gland).

Myth 3: Only middle-aged women get thyroid disease

The facts: Anyone, at any age, can develop thyroid issues, although women are five to eight times more likely to do so than men. The most common cause of hypothyroidism in the U.S. is Hashimoto’s disease, an autoimmune condition that is more common in women. Hyperthyroidism can also be caused by an autoimmune disorder called Graves’ disease, which is more common in younger women.

Myth 4: “Bulging” eyes mean you have a thyroid disorder

The facts: Bulging eyes might be caused by injury, infection, allergies, or a tumor, but some people with hyperthyroidism do indeed experience swelling of the eye muscles and tissues. This inflammation can push the eyes slightly forward, making them appear to bulge. Protruding eyes are most common in people with Graves’ disease. Eye-related thyroid issues can be treated with eye drops, medication, radiation therapy, and surgery.

Myth 5: Eating too much kale, broccoli, or other cruciferous vegetables can lead to thyroid disease

The facts: If your thyroid is healthy, eating a normal diet is highly unlikely to damage it. While it’s been thought that cruciferous vegetables can interfere with how your thyroid uses iodine, this is less likely in countries like the United States, where iodine deficiency is rare. These vegetables are packed with fiber, vitamin C, and other nutrients, and when enjoyed in moderation, the benefits far outweigh any potential for problems.

Myth 6: Supplements can help keep your thyroid healthy

The facts: The endocrine system, of which the thyroid is a key part, creates the hormones responsible for most bodily functions and self-regulates very well. While it’s important to eat a well-balanced diet with plenty of vitamins and minerals for overall health, there is no scientific evidence to prove that certain foods or supplements can improve your thyroid health. Certain supplements marketed to promote thyroid health could be harmful to some people. Ask your doctor if what you’re putting into your system for thyroid health is right for you.

Myth 7: You need extra iodine to support thyroid health

The facts: Taking excess iodine can actually be harmful for people with certain underlying thyroid conditions and anyone on thyroid medication. The normal amount of iodine that your body gets from a well-balanced diet is plenty for your thyroid to function properly. If you’re pregnant, your doctor may recommend a multivitamin that includes additional iodine to prevent deficiency. Always talk to your doctor before taking any vitamins or supplements.

Myth 8: Thyroid nodules are most likely cancerous

The facts: Thyroid nodules, or growths, are very common, especially in women, and they’re usually not cancerous. Only 5% to 10% of thyroid nodules turn out to be cancerous. They don’t show up in blood work, so they’re usually detected only when they grow large enough to be found during an examination. Ultrasound tests can help diagnose nodules, which might or might not need a biopsy depending on their size and characteristics.

The facts: Treatment for thyroid cancer is highly effective. The majority of patients diagnosed with the disease see a full recovery after surgery to remove the cancer. Usually, only monitoring is required after surgery, although patients will need to take a thyroid hormone replacement for the rest of their lives.

Ask your doctor if you think you might have a thyroid disorder

There are several different types of thyroid disorders, which affect roughly 20 million people in the U.S. These include thyroiditis (inflammation of the thyroid), hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid), hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid), thyroid nodules, and thyroid cancer. When a lump is found, there is a small risk that it will be cancerous — around 5% to 10% — but most thyroid cancers respond well to treatment.

Thankfully, there are safe, effective ways to treat thyroid disorders. Talk to your doctor if you think you might have a thyroid disorder or feel a lump in your neck.
