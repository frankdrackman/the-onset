Autism's many physical and mental health challenges can be hard to

By Dr. Breck Borcherding, for Montefiore-Einstein, Behavioral Health · 6 min read

In short

Autism awareness has led to increased visibility, acceptance and accommodation for people of all ages with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). These are our neighbors who attend school with our children, participate in scouting and other mainstream programs, are active in their churches, and on the job in many workplaces. And yet, though one in 59 children is diagnosed with ASD, we still have a ways to go to help those in our communities dealing with this lifelong condition.

Autism can affect the whole body: The interplay with other health conditions

One underappreciated challenge is the array of medical and mental health conditions that tend to be more common in people with ASD. Knowing more about the interplay between autism and other health conditions can help us understand and support these children and adults, as well as their families.

Health problems associated with autism are lifelong, affecting all age groups from early childhood through old age. More than half of people with ASD have intellectual disabilities that impact daily living, and about one-third are non-verbal. We see chronic sleep problems in more than half of autistic children, and they are eight times as likely to have gastro-intestinal problems like acid reflux and chronic constipation. Autistic children also have three to five times greater risk of seizures, often occurring during adolescence. Painful conditions that they have trouble communicating about can trigger behavioral disturbances. Dental problems, headaches, ear infections, a belly ache — all the “normal” things that effect all of us from time to time — can set off extreme behaviors, including self-injury.

A common characteristic of autism is hypo- and hyper-sensitivity to things. Hypersensitivity to sounds is one example, and even going out on a busy New York street can cause agitation and upset. Now that we understand that loud, noisy environments are a problem for people with ASD, many recreation venues have developed quieter spaces and activities where autistic kids can go and not be bothered by all the noise. This is a great example of communities addressing the needs of people with ASD.

Autistic people often have hypo-sensitivity to physical pain. This can make it hard for caregivers to recognize health issues when the person is unable to talk about it and it doesn’t present in a typical way. They might have significant gastrointestinal or dental issues, for example, where they do have some pain and need help. Again, this physical discomfort can lead to behavioral issues, which are too often mistaken for “just what goes along with autism.”

In addition to physical health challenges, people with ASD have higher rates of certain mental health issues. An estimated 30%-60% of children, and some adults, with autism suffer from attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). About 40% have anxiety problems, including avoidance and social anxiety. Those are the top two, but kids with ASD often have additional developmental disorders. Difficulties with motor development, speech and language problems, and even schizophrenia, fall into a category of neuro-developmental disorders. They are all related to each other because they tend to occur together, but also because there are genetic relationships between all these things. The combination of physical and mental challenges requires caregivers to look at multiple areas of functioning to try to figure out what each autistic child needs in terms of treatment and support. There is no one size fits all because each person with ASD has a unique combination of health challenges in addition to autism alone.

Individuals with autism can range from low- to high-functioning. At the high-functioning end of the spectrum, it can be hard to tell if there is something unique about the person unless a behavioral situation arises. Many people with ASD have trouble taking social cues, and in the case of higher-functioning children, some can be socially kind of distant while not showing any obvious problems.

But if they are placed in a situation that is unfamiliar, for example, they can become more anxious and uncomfortable and that can drive behavioral issues. How many times have all of us stood in a grocery store line thinking, “If that was my kid he wouldn’t behave that way.” But we don’t know if that child is autistic or has other developmental disabilities. How we respond as individuals and communities can make a huge difference to the lives of autistic children and their families. This is where the need for more compassion comes in.

As they mature, autistic children may outgrow some of the components of autism, and they can function very well in many situations. On the other hand, it’s not unusual for difficulties with agitation and social relationships to develop as they mature. It’s important to understand that people with ASD have the same kind of drives as everyone else for connection and intimate relationships. It’s really important for them to be given the necessary tools to successfully navigate at school, home, work and elsewhere.

For most of us, when it comes to social interactions, we just get it. In conversations, we know what to ask and what not, and how to respond to other people’s questions appropriately. But there are skills areas, like higher level math for example, where most of us would need a cheat sheet to remind us of the basic equations and how things go together. For people with ASD, social relationships and communications are like that higher level math — it doesn’t all come naturally, and there are other challenges such as repetitive movements or fixed ideas that set them apart from others. With higher-functioning people with autism, others will often see that they’re not quite right, but may attribute it to rudeness or improper upbringing rather than part of a specific syndrome. When we suspend judgment and acknowledge that maybe we don’t know what’s going on with this person, we can extend some compassion and look for ways to help.

Whether high- or low-functioning, people of all ages with ASD do best when they and their families have the acceptance and understanding of those around them, including teachers, doctors, neighbors, employers and caregivers. When behavioral issues crop up, we need to look and listen very carefully for reasons that can include medical or mental health issues, environmental conditions such as noise levels, or social pressures that are overwhelming. We can all help people with ASD to live their best possible lives in our communities.

Montefiore Einstein is a leader in the diagnosis, treatment and understanding of autism

Montefiore Einstein researchers and clinicians are at the frontlines of studying autism spectrum disorders (ASD), including genetic factors, testing novel treatments, and providing expert diagnosis and care for this complex condition. Built on a long tradition of cutting-edge work on ASD going as far back as 1958, pioneering scientists and physicians at Montefiore-Einstein have been working to understand and address the problems associated with autism. The Montefiore-Einstein Rose F. Kennedy Children’s Evaluation and Rehabilitation Center (CERC) has achieved a track record of much earlier diagnosis of autism than the national average, which benefits children because early intervention is the key to improved outcomes. The Rose F. Kennedy CERC, the Children’s Hospital at Montefiore, the Montefiore-Einstein Center for Autism and Communication Disorders and affiliated clinics offer cutting-edge evidence-based treatment options including speech and language therapies, social-skills groups, occupational and physical therapies, behavioral interventions and support for the special medical and psycho-social needs of patients with ASD across the lifespan.

Breck Borcherding, MD, is the Director of Consultation and Training for Developmental and Intellectual Disabilities at Montefiore, and an assistant professor at the Albert Einstein College of Medicine. His clinical focus is on autism and intellectual disabilities.
