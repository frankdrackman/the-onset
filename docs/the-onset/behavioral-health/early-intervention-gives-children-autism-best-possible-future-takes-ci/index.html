Early intervention gives children with autism the best possible future

By Dr. Lisa Heidi Shulman, Behavioral Health · 6 min read

In short

I’ve always agreed and promoted the idea that it takes a village to raise a typical child: The physical care, love, attention and guidance of a group of people who care for each child as an individual allows that child to grow into the best adult she or he can be. But, as a developmental pediatrician, the director of Autism Services at Montefiore’s Rose F. Kennedy Center Children’s Evaluation and Rehabilitation Center (RFK CERC), and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Act Early Ambassador Emeritus to New York, I’ll share with you that, when it comes to a child with autism and other developmental disabilities, a village isn’t enough. It truly takes a city for that child to be raised to become the best adult they can be. The first hurdle is getting a timely diagnosis.

Professionals can reliably make an autism diagnosis by 18 months of age in most children and yet, nationally, the diagnosis of autism is made at 4 years of age. Since best outcomes are seen with the receipt of the earliest intervention, that means that many children are missing out on the early intervention that could have a lifetime impact. The big question, nationally and locally, is how can we do better? Let’s go back to our city model. An early diagnosis starts with recognition that there may be a problem with how a young child is developing, communicating, interacting or playing. The early signs of autism can be quite subtle. They don’t necessarily announce themselves loudly. It is generally not the case that the child is doing unusual things that we associate with autism, but rather the art of making an early autism diagnosis involves being attuned to the usual things that a young child is not doing. Let’s go through some examples:

1. Very young babies have “conversations” — if you smile at them, they smile back. They imitate facial expressions, actions, sounds that they see and hear others carrying out around them. They are attuned to people in their environment. They are nosy and watching what other children and their parents do. They are attentive to language in their environment. When you speak to them they alert and look at you.

2. Long before their first word, young children are communicating: They use their eye contact and gestures such as pointing to let you know what they want or find interesting. They should develop 16 gestures by 16 months of age. They should also understand your gestures, such as following your finger when you direct their attention with a point.

3. Young children should be socially motivated. They should be aware of other children and adults and want to be with them. A 2-year-old has a 2-minute attention span. He should not play alone for hours on end. It is typical for a young child to want and cry for your attention (such as when you are on the phone), want your praise, and want to show you things of interest by pointing at them.

4. Young children develop pretend play skills at about 15-18 months. They feed a baby and push a toy truck. The mouthing, throwing behaviors of younger children should give way to more meaningful and organized play at this age.

5. Children whose development lags behind peers, who lose skills they had attained or plateau in their development should be evaluated.

Everyone who has contact with your child can play a role in early identification

One of the first questions I ask a family when they come to see me for a first visit is: Who referred you? Whose idea was it for your child to be evaluated? Who has concerns about this child? Is it the parent, grandparent, daycare provider, nursery school or kindergarten teacher, or pediatrician? Referrals leading to an autism diagnosis come from all of these sources, as well as the lady on the bus, the neighbor, the mother of another child at Mommy and Me. That’s the city. To identify every young child with autism early, we rely on everyone who has contact with that child to play a role.

Parents are the most important members of the referral network for a child. They know their child better than anyone else. I counsel the pediatric residents, family practitioners, nurse practitioners whom I train, to take the concern of a parent very seriously. They know their child best.

But, sometimes grandma arrives for a visit, and observes that a young toddler has not progressed as she expected since her last visit. The parents who see the child day to day may not notice something that another loving set of eyes may recognize.

Referrals that come from daycare providers, or nursery or kindergarten teachers are also very meaningful. Who is the expert on 3-year-old behavior? The experienced teacher of the 3-year-old class. She knows the range of typical skills and behaviors of 3-year-olds. If she shares with you her observations over time that your child is not talking as much as other 3-years-olds, or having trouble playing with the other children or displaying some concerning behaviors, we take that input very seriously as well.

And then there is the formal screening process of pediatricians, family practitioners, etc. Screening is the act of carrying out a standardized instrument about development on a group of children about whom no one is concerned. It’s the safety net to make sure that even if no one is concerned, we are checking to make sure that we are not missing the subtle signs of autism. The AAP recommends formal autism screening at 18 and 24 or 30 months of age for all children.

Whenever I have an opportunity to speak about autism, and even more so in the setting of the current measles epidemic, I make sure to assure parents: Vaccines do not cause autism. There is no scientific evidence to support a link between vaccines, or their ingredients, and autism.

When it comes to the causes of autism (yes there are many causes), the data suggests that anything that impacts brain development increases the likelihood of autism. There is no single autism gene, rather many genes and mutations that predispose a child to autism. While some may be passed down from a parent, others can occur at the moment of conception and may not be a pattern in other family members. Genetic testing has advanced to allow us to identify causes of autism in more children. Identifying the etiology of autism is a first step to evidence-based medical treatments for autism in addition to the current educational and therapeutic treatments.

Autism and intellectual disability are not one and the same. 50% of individuals with autism may have co-occurring intellectual disability, though 50% do not, and have cognition in the normal through the gifted range.

When it comes to autism, don’t go it alone. The identification and diagnosis of autism is the starting point. It is important that the city that helps you raise your child with autism to be their best and to help your family thrive includes professionals who can offer guidance and support to you and your child.

Montefiore’s Rose F. Kennedy Children’s Evaluation & Rehabilitation Center is an interdisciplinary service program dedicated to building the capacity of people with developmental disabilities to live full lives. With a diverse, culturally competent team, RFK CERC is on the front lines of diagnosis and treatment for autism treating more than 2,000 children with autism every year. We’re your city.

Dr. Lisa Heidi Shulman is the interim director of the Rose F. Kennedy Children’s Evaluation and Rehabilitation Center at Montefiore and Director of the RELATE program (Rehabilitation, Evaluation and Learning for Autistic Toddlers through Teens at Einstein).
