5 questions to recognize if anxiety is normal or a problem

By Simon A. Rego, Psy.D., Behavioral Health · 4 min read · Cinco preguntas para reconocer si tu ansiedad es normal o problemática

In short

Everyone feels anxious sometimes. However, when fear, worry, or a sense that something is about to go wrong becomes excessive and persistent, it can affect our ability to enjoy day-to-day relationships and activities. Chronic anxiety also increases the risk of other health conditions, including depression, substance abuse, heart disease, high blood pressure, stroke, and diabetes. That is why it is so important to learn where anxiety comes from, why we feel it, and how your doctor or mental health specialist can help you manage distressing symptoms and live your best life. The first step is asking yourself five key questions to recognize if your anxiety has become a problem.

When to take action on anxiety

Is your anxiety normal — or a problem? Ask yourself these five questions.

Unhealthy anxiety can also be hard to detect when you are experiencing it. Answering the following questions can help you know if it is time to talk to seek help.

1. Is anxiety affecting your performance at school, work, or home?

2. Is anxiety affecting your relationships or social life?

3. Is anxiety interfering with your sleep or eating patterns?

4. Is anxiety leading you to avoid doing things you normally find enjoyable or important?

5. Is the anxiety you are feeling significantly different from what you see in people experiencing the same situation?

If you answered “yes” to any of these questions, consider talking to your doctor or mental health specialist about ways to manage your anxiety. If you have what feels like an unusual amount of anxiety, remember that you are not alone and that safe, effective treatments are available.

Where does anxiety come from — and what are the symptoms?Anxiety is normal in certain situations. It is a natural emotional response to situations that are threatening or make us feel vulnerable. It can even be helpful in certain situations, motivating us to avoid harm, stay alert, or push ourselves a little harder to reach our goals. In fact, early humans relied on anxiety as a signal of imminent danger that prepared them to react quickly. While we face more psychological than physical stressors today, our brains still respond to stressors in the same way: Our bodies go into a fight-or-flight response. A cascade of hormones throws the body into high alert, sending blood to the heart and muscles in preparation for physical combat or escape. What combination of treatments might be right for you?

Remember that you are not alone and that safe, effective treatments are available, including:

Cognitive behavioral therapy: Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), a clinically proven, skills-based form of talk therapy, is considered by experts to be the most effective psychotherapy for treating anxiety. It works by teaching people how to recognize, challenge, and change their anxious thoughts and correct any problematic behavior patterns.

Lifestyle changes: Even people with occasional or mild anxiety can feel better by adding stress-management strategies to their daily routine. Yoga, mindfulness meditation, and aerobic exercise — such as running, brisk walking, or dancing — are all good for your general well-being and help calm the mind. Simple steps like eating healthy foods, getting a good night’s sleep each night, and avoiding alcohol and recreational drugs also help reduce vulnerability to stress and anxiety.

Medications: Prescription medications can be used in combination with psychotherapy or on their own. Fast-acting medicines that calm the nervous system can be taken occasionally to quickly alleviate symptoms of acute anxiety or a panic attack. Drugs that have been shown to help with anxiety over the long term target certain neurotransmitters in the brain and can help reduce anxious thoughts and feelings.
