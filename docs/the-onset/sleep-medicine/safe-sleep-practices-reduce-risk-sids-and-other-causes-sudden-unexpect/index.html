Safe sleep practices reduce the risk of SIDS and other causes of

By Dr. Deborah Campbell, for Montefiore, Sleep Medicine · 6 min read

In short

For about 3,600 American families each year, the joy of a new baby in the home is cut short by sudden unexpected infant death (SUID). Some of these tragic incidents are attributed to Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS), which is the death of an apparently healthy baby which cannot be explained after a thorough examination of all possible causes. Many are the result of accidental strangulation or suffocation in their sleep space. Others cannot be classified because there was not an exhaustive investigation of possible causes. Most of these heartbreaking losses occur between the ages of 1 and 4 months, with 90% occurring before 6 months of age. While the cause of SIDS is the subject of ongoing research, we know how to reduce the risk and keep babies safe from deadly accidents while they sleep.

The American Academy of Pediatrics, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and a multitude of other organizations, including the Children’s Hospital at Montefiore, have shared guidelines for safe sleep for infants. The number one recommendation is that every infant be put to sleep on his or her back, unless explicitly instructed otherwise by the baby’s doctor. This includes high-risk babies, pre-term babies and babies with reflux or those who spit up frequently. Here are the guidelines to create a safe sleep space for baby:

• The baby should sleep in the same room as the parents, but in a separate space such as a bassinet or crib, for at least the first six months and up to one year.

• The mattress in the crib or bassinet should be firm and covered with a tightly fitted sheet.

• Cribs should have slats that are spaced so that a standard soda can cannot pass between them — about 2.5 inches is fine.

• No bumpers, pillows or toys in the sleep space, and no blankets. A sleep sack or stretchy sleeper garment will be enough to keep the baby warm and comfortable.

• Do not use positioners. Babies have turned over and been trapped in them.

• Offer the baby a pacifier at bedtime and nap time. This may prevent SIDS, though we don’t know why. If you are breastfeeding, wait until your baby is about a month old before giving a pacifier.

• Keep the room below 70 degrees. Don’t overdress the baby to avoid overheating.

Our understanding of the importance of safe sleep practices continues to grow, and some of what has been learned may run contrary to a family’s traditions and practices. Parents and any others who will care for the baby should know and follow these current guidelines for safe sleep to significantly reduce your baby’s risk of SIDS or SUID.

• Number one priority is to place the baby on her back to sleep, which takes advantage of her anatomy to protect from choking. The trachea, which is the child’s breathing tube, sits in front of the esophagus, which goes to the stomach. If the baby is asleep on her back and spits up, she is going to swallow it back down into her stomach. If she’s on her stomach, there is a greater risk that she’ll swallow whatever came up into her airway and choke.

• Many people believe that babies are more comfortable and sleep more soundly on their sides or stomachs. They do, but part of the reason for that, especially if their faces are down, is that they’re re-breathing their carbon dioxide. While this may put them into a deeper sleep state, it is not considered safe for the baby.

• There are many, many night wakings to feed a young infant, and often times the baby ends up in the adult bed, where both parent and baby may fall asleep. That’s why it’s important that parents who are doing night feedings in bed prepare to keep the baby safe. The bed should be firm with no pillows, blankets or comforters the baby could become trapped or tangled in. If you fall asleep unintentionally, place the baby back in the crib or bassinet as soon as you wake up. Never, ever put a baby between yourself and your partner in the bed to sleep.

• Babies may fall asleep in a variety of locations throughout the day such as car seats, bouncy seats and swings. Parents sometimes hesitate to wake the baby to move him to his crib, but it is important to know that these locations are not safe sleep spaces for the baby. In addition, hammocks and bean bags for infants have recently come on the market, and they should be completely avoided. A young infant can sink into them, get stuck, and flex their neck inappropriately. Babies should not sleep on adult beds, sofas, recliners, chairs, or any soft surface, including water beds or soft mattresses. The best place for your baby to sleep is always going to be the safe sleep place you have prepared for him — with a firm mattress, and no bumpers, loose bedding, blankets, pillows or toys.

SIDS prevention starts during the prenatal period, because we know that the healthier the mother is, the lower the risk of sudden infant death. Reducing the chance of having a pre-term or low birthweight baby, and avoiding exposure of the fetus to drugs and alcohol, are early important steps. Babies whose mothers smoke during pregnancy and/or are exposed to cigarette smoke after birth have a higher risk of SIDS. Never smoke near an infant — even outdoors.

Breastfeeding is another way to reduce the risk of SIDS. Studies have shown that breastfeeding for as little as two months reduces the risk of SIDS by 50%. Breastfeeding does not need to be exclusive for a baby to be protected so that babies who are breastfeeding and receiving infant formula are protected as well. In addition to the health benefits of breast milk, it has been found that both mother and infant are more easily arousable. Research shows that particularly with infants who are at risk of Sudden Infant Death Syndrome, part of the cause is suspected to be structural abnormalities of the brain that make the brain less responsive and less arousable. So if you have an infant in a more arousable state, and a mother, that also reduces the risk.

Keeping the room on the cool side — below 70 degrees — is also protective by helping to prevent the baby from overheating during sleep. Babies need only a comfortable stretchy sleeper or a sleep sack to stay warm. Don’t over bundle the baby or use blankets in the crib.

Montefiore is part of the New York State Perinatal Quality Collaborative, which supports safe sleep education in the hospital setting and beyond. In addition to outreach efforts to families, it works with retailers who sell infant bedding to educate them on appropriately modeling safe sleep environments in displays. This long-established program has been integral to educating parents about safe sleep for infants.

For families who have lost a baby and don’t have an identifiable cause, Montefiore’s Center for CardioGenetics offers evaluation and genetic testing for underlying medical conditions or health risks that may have predisposed the child to sudden death, and which may impact other family members.

Montefiore is committed to helping families overcome any financial barriers to safe sleep for their infants. A crib or bassinet is the foundation of safe sleep, and we can help parents access the federally funded Cribs for Kids program to address this need. The Health Alliance of Hudson Valley, in partnership with New York State’s Healthy Start program also provides access to low-cost or free sleep resources.

Safe sleep practices have been embraced by millions of American families, substantially reducing the incidence of SIDS and preventing sleep-related accidental deaths of infants. At Montefiore, we’re committed to helping your baby sleep safe every night, and nap time too.

Deborah E. Campbell, M.D., is Director of the Division of Neonatology, Program Director, Neonatal-Perinatal Medicine, and Director, Low birth weight infant Evaluation and Assessment Program (LEAP) at the Children’s Hospital of Montefiore. Dr. Campbell is also Professor of Pediatrics and Associate Professor of Obstetrics & Gynecology and Women’s Health at the Albert Einstein College of Medicine.
